# 119 - Milon's Secret Castle

Hudson Soft made and published this 1988 platformer, in which a bubble-blowing kid goes into a castle to rescue a princess. The back of the box can't decide whether it is Milon's Secret Castle or Hudson's Secret Castle, and the castle is actually called Castle Garland. We were lost for most of it. The game is a collection of answers you will never find, with no questions to go with them.

### How it works

You walk around a castle hub, go into rooms, and run, jump and blow bubbles, your only attack. You hunt for money and clues that lead to the bosses locking off each floor. The hub is thin, mostly poking in and out of doors. Metroid and Super Mario Bros. 3 got far more out of their hubs and maps.

### What doesn't

- **Guess and check.** In the first level the money is hidden in the walls with nothing marking it, and the blocks all look alike, unlike Mario's three kinds. It comes down to shooting everything until you find what you're looking for.
- **The shoes.** The shop that sells the shoes sits behind a block you push by running into it for a few seconds, then shooting the blank space behind it. The shoes let you use springs, and then you need a shrinking potion and a boxing glove. We only know because we looked it up.
- **The boss.** It waits in a window on the overworld, and the arena can look empty, like a glitch. The first boss is a bat-winged dog you hit in the head.
- **Taking damage.** There are no invincibility frames, so touching anything hurts until you move. Linger on the overworld and a thunderstorm chases you, even hitting you during a door animation when you have no control.

### Worth knowing

A honeybee hidden in every stage flies away once you find it, and it is the Hudson Soft logo. We also found a fan site, Milon's Secret Website, with every level graphed on graph paper, and we called it beautiful that someone cared this much.

### The bottom line

Skip it. Nobody voted it onto the Essential Games List. Joe praised the look and some enemy designs but called it "nowhere near an essential game" and wouldn't give it a play either. His theory is that talented people made the puzzles, the answers and the assets and were not allowed to speak to each other. Sean's best praise was "a silky smooth 60 frames per second." We agreed a good game may be hidden in there, but a ROM hack that spells things out might not make it more fun.
