# 453 - Whomp 'Em

- UNKNOWN PERIL LURKS BEHIND EVERY CORNER

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: a kid with a spear collects seven magic totems, and that is the entire story
  - You are Soaring Eagle, described in the manual as a young Indian Brave venturing out to gather totems for his magic pouch.
  - There is no cutscene, no crawl, no text in the game itself. The premise exists only in the manual.
- The identity problem, and it is a big one
  - This is a reskin of a Japanese game called Saiyuki World 2: Tenjokai no Majin, released on Famicom on December 7, 1990.
  - In that version you play Sun Wukong, the Monkey King from Journey to the West. Same game, same levels, same bosses.
  - Jaleco swapped the monkey for a Native American boy, the staff for a spear, and shipped it in North America in March 1991.
  - And it gets weirder going backward: Saiyuki World 2 is a sequel to Saiyuki World, which was itself Jaleco and NMK's heavily altered Famicom version of Sega's Wonder Boy in Monster Land. (Hardcore Gaming 101)
  - So the chain is Wonder Boy to Monkey King to Native American, across three companies and two continents.
- The simplest way to frame it for listeners
  - This is Mega Man with a spear.
  - Intro stage, then six stages in any order, a new power from each boss, then a final gauntlet.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The reskin is visible from the first screen, and it never stops being visible
  - Soaring Eagle runs through bamboo forests. He fights samurai. He punches out things drawn straight out of East Asian folklore.
  - "bamboo forests are not native to the United States, Jaleco," and that is the whole experience.
  - The only enemy sprite Jaleco bothered to redraw was a panda midboss, which became a brown bear. In the bamboo forest.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Eight stages, six of them in whatever order you want
  - World 1 is a bossless introductory stage. Beat it and a map screen opens up.
  - Six selectable stages: Sacred Woods, Magic Forest, Water Test, Secret Cliff, Ice Ritual, Fire Test. (Manual)
  - Clear all six and the final secret stage unlocks in the center of the map.
- Basic controls (they're simple, which is part of the charm)
  - D-pad: move, duck (hold Down), raise the spear overhead (hold Up).
  - A: jump.
  - B: thrust the spear forward.
  - Up + jump: stab upward. Down + jump: stab downward, and you can land on enemies with it.
  - Start: cycle through your collected totems.
  - Select: pause, and it also shows how many more gourds you need for your next heart. (GameFAQs, manual)
- The combat loop is stab and reposition
  - Enemies take one to four hits depending on type. Gremlins have shields and are only vulnerable while throwing daggers. Snails are invincible in the shell.
  - The downstab is your best friend. Vertical sections are built around it.
  - Every enemy you kill drops something, so grinding is not a chore, it's the health system.
- The item loop is the gourd economy
  - Gourds are the most common drop. Collect enough and your maximum health permanently goes up by one heart.
  - You start with four hearts and cap at twelve.
  - The gourd requirement scales brutally: 9 gourds for your fifth heart, 12 for the sixth, 22 for the eighth, and 80 for the twelfth. (GameFAQs)
  - Crucially, your gourd count and heart maximum carry over even when you continue. That is the real progression system in this game.
  - Also in the mix: small hearts (heal one), large hearts (heal all), flint spear head (attack up, four hits), buffalo headdress (defense up, four hits), deerskin shirt (brief invincibility), and a spear pickup that extends your reach for the rest of the stage. (Manual, GameFAQs)
- Magic potions are your lives, and they work automatically
  - Hold up to three. Run out of health and one triggers on its own, restoring you mid-fight.
  - Lose all potions and all hearts and it's game over, but you get unlimited continues back to the stage select.
  - Hidden potions are scattered throughout every level in specific spots, which is a Wonder Boy holdover: jump in the right place and things pop out of thin air. (Hardcore Gaming 101)

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- The totems are the Mega Man hook, and most of them are bad
  - Spear Whirlwind (Sacred Woods): repels projectiles and breaks rocks. Mostly a key, not a weapon.
  - Fire Wand (Fire Test): flame from the spear tip. Melts ice blocks, wrecks the Ice Guardian. Probably the best of the six.
  - Cloud (Ice Ritual): summon a cloud and ride it. The GameFAQs writer's complaint is that it takes forever to form, drifts away while you chase it, and then moves slowly.
  - Ice Crystal (Water Test): freezes enemies. Useful, but you usually could have just killed them.
  - Web (Magic Forest): trap an enemy and throw it at another enemy. Works on very few enemy types. Ranked dead last by the GameFAQs writer.
  - Dart (Secret Cliff): throw spears that stick in walls and become platforms. Weak as an attack, genuinely interesting as a traversal tool.
  - Be honest here: multiple reviewers independently landed on "the special weapons are almost useless until the final level forces them on you."
- Unlimited use, which is the interesting design divergence from Mega Man
  - No weapon energy bar. Once you have a totem, you can spam it forever. (GameFAQs)
  - That sounds generous, but it removes the resource-management tension that made Mega Man's weapon swapping feel like a decision.
  - It also means the totems have to be good on their own merits, and mostly they aren't.
- The seventh totem
  - The manual lists six totems with names and descriptions, then a seventh entry that just reads: "(?) Unknown: You will have to find out the power of this totem on your own!"
  - It's the Death Branch, awarded automatically after all six bosses fall. It fires a dragon head that destroys everything in its path.
  - The catch: it costs you one full heart of health every time you fire it.
  - Which means the best weapon in the game runs on your own life bar, and at max health you get eleven shots before you're down to your last heart.
- The final stage is a different game
  - Every enemy in the last level is a miniature version of one of the six bosses, retooled and meaner. That's a genuinely cool idea.
  - It's also the longest level, it forces you to use every totem, and there is an anti-gravity section where your only thrust is your spear attack. Multiple writers describe those controls as awful.
  - The final boss is where everyone agrees the game breaks. He has an attack that hits you anywhere on screen, which is not communicated at all, and the accepted strategy is to trade hearts with the Death Branch until he dies.

## V. History & Development: The Reskin

- The name
  - "Whomp 'Em" is a pun on wampum, the shell beads used by Eastern Woodlands nations.
- Reception was quiet
  - Nintendo Power: 3.1 out of 5, May 1991 issue 24 (Wikipedia).
  - Famitsu: 23 out of 40 for the Japanese version (Wikipedia).
  - AllGame: 3.5 out of 5, with Weiss praising the graphics, music, and controls.
  - It essentially vanished, then got rehabilitated decades later. 1UP's "Hidden Gems: The Rest of the NES" feature is the citation Wikipedia leans on, and that hidden-gem reputation drove the cart price up. Take on the NES Library noted picking up copies for eight to ten dollars before the market caught on, with loose carts running around thirty-five dollars by 2018. Current listings run higher.
- It finally got a legitimate rerelease in 2025
  - City Connection put out a two-in-one JALECOlle package on Switch on August 7, 2025, containing both Whomp 'Em and Saiyuki World 2.
  - That release included the first official English translation of Saiyuki World 2, which means for the first time you can play the original and the reskin side by side.
  - Bonus features include rewind, quick save, an attack info screen, and a gourd counter, which tells you they knew the gourd math was opaque.

## VI. Music & Presentation

- The composer signed the game with a joke name
  - Tsukasa Tawada wrote the music. In Whomp 'Em he is credited as "Adios Amigo."
  - According to the Video Game Music Preservation Foundation, he picked it because "Adios" sounds like "Audio." He has said nobody made him or the other developers use aliases, they just did it to be funny.
  - He also credited himself as "Ludwig Tawada" on another Jaleco game, a Beethoven joke.
- And then he went on to score Pokémon
  - Tawada composed Pokémon Colosseum in 2003, then Pokémon XD: Gale of Darkness, Pokémon Trozei, and Pokémon Battle Revolution.
  - The man credited as Adios Amigo on a reskinned Famicom platformer wrote one of the most beloved soundtracks in the Pokémon spinoff catalog.
  - NEStalgia callback: Tawada also did Moon Crystal, which we covered on our bytes episode

## VII. Pop Culture

- The timing is almost too on the nose
  - Whomp 'Em hit North America in March 1991. Dances with Wolves won seven Academy Awards including Best Picture on March 25, 1991, the first Western to take Best Picture since 1931.
  - Jaleco was localizing this in late 1990 and early 1991, exactly when American mainstream culture was having its biggest Native-American-themed moment in decades.
- "One of the first Native American video game protagonists," with an asterisk
  - The Turtle Island Games Archive, a Texas Christian University project cataloging depictions of Indigenous peoples in games, says in one section that Whomp 'Em is the first game with a Native American protagonist, then hedges elsewhere that it's only cited as potentially the first.
- Where the "spirit quest platformer" idea went next
  - The 1990s gave us Chief Thunder in Killer Instinct and T. Hawk in Super Street Fighter II, both broad fighting-game archetypes.
  - Turok on Nintendo 64 in 1997 is the first Native protagonist with real commercial weight behind him.
  - Nearly twenty-five years after Whomp 'Em, Never Alone was built in collaboration with Iñupiaq storytellers, which is the actual answer to what this could have been.

## VIII. Sequels, Spinoffs & Comparison Games

- Comparison games
  - Mega Man 2: the structural template, right down to stage select and boss powers. The difference is that Mega Man's weapons are decisions and Whomp 'Em's are mostly tools.
  - Zelda II: The Adventure of Link: the up-and-down aerial spear thrusts are the direct comparison, and HG101 suspects that's where they came from.
  - The Krion Conquest: HG101's own reference point for how far a Mega Man clone can go. Krion is the blatant one, Whomp 'Em just borrows the shape.
  - Totally Rad: another Jaleco game that got heavily reworked for America. Good companion piece if you want a "what was Jaleco's localization department doing" segment.
  - Bionic Commando: for the gourd system specifically, which works like the bullet collection that upgrades your health bar.

## IX. Essential Games List Vote

- Case for Essential
  - It is one of the best-playing NES platformers that almost nobody played, and the stage-select structure gives it real replay value that most late-era action games don't have.
  - Supporting: the controls are tight, the eight-direction spear work is genuinely good, and the persistent heart upgrades make repeat runs feel productive.
  - Supporting: it's a perfect artifact of what the NES library actually was in 1991, which is imported games in American costumes.
- Case against Essential
  - The story of this game is more interesting than the game, and an essential list should be about the playing, not the trivia.
  - Supporting: six of the seven special weapons are widely agreed to be useless, which guts the Mega Man structure it's borrowing.
  - Supporting: the final boss is cheap enough that the accepted strategy is trading health for damage until he dies, which is not a fight, it's arithmetic.
  - Supporting: if you want the stage-select action platformer experience on NES, Mega Man 2 through 4 are right there and they are better.
