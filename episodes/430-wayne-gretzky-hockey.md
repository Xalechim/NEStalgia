# 430 - Wayne Gretzky Hockey

- Endorsed by the greatest name in Hockey

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: It's 1991. Wayne Gretzky — the greatest hockey player who ever lived, mid-dynasty, already on the Kings — puts his name on an NES cartridge. The game promises realistic simulation hockey with full coaching options, multiple game speeds, animated refs, and body checks.
- Developed by Bethesda Softworks. Published by THQ. Released January 1991. Read that developer name again. Bethesda Softworks, founded in 1986 by Christopher Weaver in Bethesda, Maryland.
- The same Bethesda that would create The Elder Scrolls. The same Bethesda that would publish Fallout 3, Skyrim, Starfield. Before they were building open-world RPGs that consumed thousands of hours of people's lives, they were making sports sims — and the NES version of Wayne Gretzky Hockey was reputedly coded in a single night.
- Bethesda published the game shortly after Peter Pocklington traded Gretzky from the Edmonton Oilers to the Los Angeles Kings on August 9, 1988. a trade that shook Canada to its core. The PC version came first in 1988 and was well-received. The NES port arrived three years later..

## II. What Makes It Notable

- The overhead view and sim ambitions mixed
- The game uses a top-down perspective, unlike any other sports game we’ve seen so far.
- With fewer pixels and a smaller viewport, it creates a zoomed-out, cluttered mess where players are tiny and the puck is even smaller
- Options include multiple game speeds and difficulty levels, a practice mode, and adjustable period lengths.
- On paper, the feature set is deep for an NES sports game. Exhibition, playoffs, full seasons. Penalties, icings, offsides. The problem isn't what the game offers — it's how it plays
- The player-switching catastrophe broken
- If a player on your team gets the puck, the game doesn't auto-switch you to that player. By the time you've mashed Select enough times to cycle through your entire team and switch to the guy that has the puck, he's since shot it into a wall or passed it to the other team.
- Every NES hockey game before this understood that when the puck changes possession, you need to be controlling the player who has it. Ice Hockey does it. Blades of Steel does it. Wayne Gretzky Hockey makes you manually cycle through your roster while the AI drives your puck carrier into the boards.
- The AI broken
- The AI is so stupid it's staggering. Your computer-controlled teammates don't play hockey
- They don't position themselves for passes, they don't support offensive rushes, they don't defend with any awareness.
- Combined with the player-switching problem, you're essentially fighting your own team as much as the opponent
- The NES flickering feels dated
- The NES flickering can make it especially hard to parse what's going on when the players on both teams scramble for the puck all at once.
- With ten skaters plus goalies on a small NES screen in a top-down view, sprite flickering is constant. The puck disappears into pileups. Players blink in and out of existence.
- The cartridge variants collector note
- The game exists in at least three cartridge label variants: a black Kings jersey version, a white Kings jersey version, and a white jersey with no THQ logo. The white jersey variant is notably rarer and commands higher prices in the collector market. Same game on all of them — the only difference is which Gretzky you see on the label. It's a minor curiosity, but for NES collectors it's one of the more interesting variant stories in the library

## III. Pop Culture, Legacy & Connections

- Bethesda's origin story:
- Bethesda is credited with the creation of the first physics-based sports simulation, Gridiron!, in 1986.
- Electronic Arts was working on the first John Madden Football and hired Bethesda to help finish developing it.
- The next year, Bethesda sued EA for $7.3 million, claiming EA halted the release of Gridiron! while incorporating many of its elements into Madden. The case was resolved out of court.
- Konami used a Wayne Gretzky–Thomas Jonsson photo from the 1983 Stanley Cup Finals as the basis for the Blades of Steel box art. Altering the likenesses and uniforms. So even on the game that wasn't named after him, Gretzky's image was selling hockey. The man was unavoidable. He was the sport
