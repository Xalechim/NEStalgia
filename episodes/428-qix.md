# 428 - Qix

- YOUR BEST DEFENSE IS A MENTAL OFFENSE

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: You control a diamond-shaped marker on the edge of an empty rectangle. Your job is to draw lines into the void, carve out territory, and claim at least 75% of the screen. The only thing stopping you is the Qix — a writhing, colorful geometric figure drifting randomly through the open space — along with Sparx patrolling the borders and a fuse that lights up your unfinished line if you stop moving
- Originally released in arcades in October 1981 by Taito America. Designed by husband-and-wife team Randy and Sandy Pfeiffer. The NES port was developed by Novotrade (Ecco the dolphin) and published by Taito in January 1991 — a full decade after the arcade original
- The origin story: Randy created the Qix's movement as a simple programming demo. Sandy suggested making a game out of it — "sort of like video-game Etch A Sketch." Their eureka moment came in a jacuzzi with a bottle of vintage champagne. Ginger Beard Man The game was named after Randy's license plate at the time: JUS4QIX, pronounced "Just For Kicks"
- The central thing worth dwelling on: this is a game with no characters, no narrative, no world. Just geometry, tension, and greed. Every single decision is a risk-reward calculation expressed as a line on a screen. Nothing else on the NES plays like this, and almost nothing in the arcade did either

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The slow draw / fast draw system holds up
- Two buttons, one decision. Slow draw fills your claimed territory in red/orange and scores double points. Fast draw fills in blue, scores less, but gets you back to the safety of a border faster. Every line you draw is a bet — how long can you stay exposed in the open field before the Qix drifts into your path or a Sparx catches up?
- This is the entire game. One binary input that creates infinite gradients of risk. It's elegant in a way that most NES games never even attempt. The scoring system isn't decoration — it's the reason to play. Safe players will clear stages but never post meaningful scores. Greedy players will post huge numbers but die constantly. The game lives in the space between those two impulses
- The slow draw as a design concept is essentially "the game pays you to be afraid longer." That's a remarkable piece of psychology for 1981
- The Qix itself holds up
- The Qix doesn't chase you. It doesn't know you exist. It's just a cluster of lines performing random, graceful, unpredictable motion inside the rectangle. You can't kill it, can't predict it, can't negotiate with it. You can only watch it and hope
- The Qix is not a villain. It's weather. And that distinction is what makes the game feel different from everything else on the console. There's no pattern to memorize, no AI to exploit. Just chaos that you have to draw around
- The design implication: because the Qix is random, no two games play the same way. Every session is a new negotiation with entropy. That's a quality most NES games — built on memorization and pattern recognition — never offer
- The Sparx and the fuse holds up
- Sparx travel along every completed border — your claimed edges, the outer walls, everything. They're the ticking clock that prevents you from sitting safely on the perimeter forever. A meter at the top counts down to the release of additional Sparx and eventually Super Sparx, which can chase you along your unfinished line
- The fuse is the cruelest mechanic: if you stop drawing mid-line, a fuse lights and burns toward your marker. Hesitation is death. The game literally punishes you for freezing
- And then there's the Spiral Death Trap — draw yourself into a corner where you can't proceed in any direction and you're dead. The attract mode actually names it. The game warns you about a way to die that most players will discover on their own, usually once, usually memorably
- The two-Qix split holds up
- Starting at level three, the screen contains two Qixes. If you draw a line that divides the playfield so each Qix is trapped in a separate region, the level ends immediately — and a score multiplier is permanently applied to every subsequent level
- This is the high-level play that separates tourists from lifers. Splitting the Qixes is enormously risky — you're drawing a line across the entire screen with two random entities drifting around — but the compounding multiplier makes it the only strategy that matters for serious score runs. It's the kind of mechanic that reveals itself slowly and then redefines the game once you understand it
- The NES port specifically mixed
- The port is faithful to the arcade — the core mechanics are intact and play correctly. Electronic Gaming Monthly listed the NES version at #100 on their "100 Best Games of All Time" list in 1997, specifically for its risk-versus-reward system Wikipedia
- The honest trade-off: the visuals are spartan even by NES standards. The playfield is mostly empty rectangles and solid color fills. There's essentially no music during gameplay — just sound effects and silence. By 1991, NES players were used to Mega Man 3 and Castlevania III. Qix looks and sounds like it arrived from another era, because it did
- But the argument for the port: nothing about Qix needs audiovisual polish. The tension comes entirely from the mechanics. A prettier version wouldn't be a better version. The emptiness is the point — it keeps your attention on the line, the Qix, and the shrinking margin between safety and disaster

## III. Pop Culture, Legacy & Connections

- The creators vanished: Randy Pfeiffer moved on from the video game industry to run his own business creating CAD software for model railway enthusiasts. When a fan tracked him down in 2003, he was the first person outside of Taito to contact Pfeiffer about the game — the first in over twenty years. Ginger Beard Man A husband-and-wife team invented one of the most influential puzzle games ever made, and then walked away entirely. The game became more famous than its creators, a parallel the interviewer explicitly drew to Tetris
- Kaneko released Gals Panic in 1990, an adult-oriented Qix variant where completing stages progressively revealed images of Japanese models in various states of undress. Giant Bomb It spawned numerous sequels and clones, and is considered the originator of the "reveal-a-picture" eroge subgenre. Wikipedia Qix's core mechanic — "claim territory to reveal what's underneath" — turned out to be a perfect engine for a type of game its creators never imagined. Den of Geek included Qix on a list of the top 10 most cloned video games. Wikipedia A significant percentage of those clones are explicitly pornographic. That's a legacy worth talking about honestly
- The Game Boy port, developed by Minakuchi Engineering and published by Nintendo, featured intermission scenes with Mario, Luigi, and Princess Peach cameos Wikipedia — including one where Mario appears in a desert wearing Mexican clothing and playing a guitar with a vulture looking on. That outfit later appeared as a wearable costume in Super Mario Odyssey. Wikipedia A Qix port on Game Boy may have quietly contributed to Mario's wardrobe decades later
- Taito's own Director of Creativity, Keith Egging, admitted that after an initial commercial hit, the game faded because it was "conceptually too mystifying for gamers" and "impossible to master." Wikipedia That's the developer's own postmortem — the game was too abstract, too open, too weird to sustain mainstream interest in 1981. The question is whether that's a failure of the game or a failure of the audience. By modern indie standards, Qix's minimalism and mechanical purity would be celebrated. It was ahead of its time in a way that actively hurt it
- Thematic relatives:
- Tetris — the closest spiritual comparison. Both are abstract puzzle games with no narrative, infinite play, and a core loop built on managing increasing pressure. Tetris won the culture war; Qix didn't.
- Worth asking why Tempest (1981, Atari) — another abstract arcade game from the same year that turned geometry into anxiety. Both prove that visual abstraction and mechanical depth aren't in tension Snake — the "draw a line, don't hit anything" concept reduced to its most primitive form. Qix is the sophisticated version of an impulse that's been in games forever
- Baba Is You, HyperRogue, and the modern abstract puzzle lineage — games that prove the audience Qix needed eventually showed up, just thirty years late

## IV. Closing Bits

- The question to leave listeners with: Qix has no ending. There's no final boss, no credit roll, no conclusion. You play until you die and the only record is a high score. In a medium that now treats narrative completion as table stakes, is there still room for a game that offers nothing but a score and the knowledge of how you got it? Is the absence of an ending a design flaw or the most honest statement a game can make?
