# 457 - World Champ

- Can you deliver the crushing blow to become WORLD CHAMP? (Note: this is the closing line of the back-of-box copy, which opens "You enter the arena to the sound of thunderous applause." Source: Rarity Club box listing)

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: a boxing game where you train your fighter before you fight
  - There is no story. You are a boxer, you want the belt, and you climb the ladder to get it.
  - It is a career mode with light RPG elements, which is unusual for a boxing game of this era.
- The game has a few names and a few makers
  - In Japan it was Great Boxing: Rush Up, released December 7, 1990. North America got World Champ from Romstar in April 1991. Europe got it from Taito as World Champ: Super Boxing Great Fight. (GameFAQs release data, MobyGames)
  - Who made it depends on who you ask. Wikipedia's Visco page lists it as a Visco game with no citation. GameFAQs and most review sites say Pixel developed it, with Visco publishing in Japan. 
  - The Japanese version has thinly veiled real boxers: Ray Leonard, Roberto Duran, Marvin Hagler, and "Iron Mike" as the final heavyweight. The Western release swaps them for generic names like "Action Jim" and "Jumbo Joe."
- The simplest way to frame it for listeners
  - This is not Punch-Out!!. There are no pattern-memorization puzzle fights.
  - It is closer to Data East's Ring King: top-down, stat building, a ladder of opponents. 

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- Training is the hook
  - Before you fight, you build four abilities: speed, strength, stamina, and defense. 
  - Training works through sparring matches against lower-ranked fighters in your division, and those matches play just like real ones.
  - Most NES boxing games of the era skip this, which is what separates World Champ from them.
- Spirit and stamina, two meters
  - Stamina determines how fast you go down. Less stamina means a quicker count.
  - Spirit rises when you land hits and falls when you take them. Once it is in the red, any punch can put your opponent on the canvas.
  - That gives the fights a momentum feel: land enough, then the knockdown is yours to take.
- Modes
  - Ranking: the one-player career, with four weight classes.
  - Versus: a 2-player match.
  - Tournament: up to 8 players pass the controller in a bracket. A nice party feature for 1991.
  - The credits shout out '80s glam-metal acts: Alice Cooper, Hanoi Rocks, and Mötley Crüe. Nobody has an explanation for why a boxing game is thanking Hanoi Rocks.
  - The referee voice, shrill and piercing.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- The career loop
  - Pick a boxer, then train, fight, and move up the rankings in your weight class.
  - There are twenty increasingly difficult opponents across four weight classes.
  - The password list names Welter, Middle, Cruiser, and Heavy, so the ladder runs from welterweight up to heavyweight.
- Basic controls
  - D-pad: move around the ring. The direction also picks the punch you throw.
  - Up plus attack: uppercut.
  - Down plus attack: body blow.
  - Left or right plus attack: straight punch.
  - A throws punches and B defends (block and weave).
  - Holding the defend button works, but bobbing and weaving around your opponent turns out to be easier than standing still and blocking.
- POW points
  - A pre-fight screen shows four moves: Straight Punch, Body Blow, Uppercut, and Sudden Hook. You assign POW points to them to tune your fighter.
  - That is the RPG layer. You are building a boxer, not just playing as one.
- Combat loop
  - It plays like Rock'Em Sock'Em Robots: land consecutive hits to pin your opponent, then finish with combos.
  - Movement and punching feel smooth and responsive. That is a real plus on the NES.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- Sparring that counts
  - Practice fights use the same rules as real ones, so training is a small game of its own.
- Bob and weave beats block
  - The defense button exists, but players find evasion more effective. If blocking is the weaker option, why include it?
- Passwords
  - Five passwords jump you ahead (GameFAQs cheats page):
  - SWJMNWGBX: start at cruiserweight with max power.
  - DZLMNZLMN: start at heavyweight with max power.
  - SZLMNZLMN: start at middleweight with max power.
  - QZLMNZLMN: start at welterweight with max everything.
  - BZLMNZLMN: jump to the final match.
  - All the codes look nearly identical, which suggests a very simple password scheme.
- The button-masher complaint
  - Is it a button-masher with limited depth. Is that fair? Once your stats are maxed, do fights lose their tension?

## V. Pop Culture: The Boxing Scene in 1990-91

- The timing is the story: World Champ launched in the middle of the strangest stretch in heavyweight history.
- The real-world fights
  - Feb 11, 1990: Buster Douglas knocks out Mike Tyson in the tenth round in Tokyo. Tyson had been undefeated and the champ for nearly four years. (Wikipedia)
  - Oct 25, 1990: Evander Holyfield knocks out Douglas in the third round to become undisputed champion. Douglas's reign lasted about eight months. (Wikipedia: Douglas vs. Holyfield)
  - Mar 18, 1991: Tyson vs. Razor Ruddock I. The ref stops it in round seven and the crowd riots over the call. Tyson wins the rematch on June 28, 1991, by decision. (Wikipedia: Tyson vs. Ruddock)
  - Apr 1991: World Champ reaches North America, within weeks of Holyfield vs. 41-year-old George Foreman, "The Battle of the Ages," on April 19. (Wikipedia: Holyfield vs. Foreman)
  - Tyson vs. Holyfield was being set up for November 1991, until Tyson was arrested that summer.
- Rocky V
  - Nov 16, 1990: Rocky V hits theaters. Rocky retires after the Drago fight, trains Tommy Gunn, and it all ends with a street fight. Rotten Tomatoes has it at 31%, though audiences gave it an A CinemaScore, and Stallone later rated it 0/10. (Wikipedia)
- Boxing games from the same window
  - James "Buster" Douglas Knockout Boxing (Genesis): Sega signed Douglas right after the upset and released the game in the US on June 1, 1990, a few months after Douglas beat Tyson. It was a reskin of Taito's arcade game Final Blow (1989). (Wikipedia: Final Blow, Sega Does)
    - Reviews were poor. Mega magazine put it on a "10 worst Mega Drive games" list. (Wikipedia: Final Blow)
  - Final Blow (arcade, 1989): Taito's side-view arcade boxer. It reached North America in June 1989 through Romstar, the same publisher that brought us World Champ. (Wikipedia: Final Blow)
  - Heavyweight Championship Boxing / Boxing (Game Boy, 1990): by Tose, released May 18, 1990 in Japan and September 1990 in North America, published by Activision there. Players distribute points across health, stamina, and speed, which is very close to World Champ's stat-building idea. (Wikipedia: Boxing (1990 video game))
    - So the "build your boxer" idea was showing up on more than one platform that year.
- Talking points
  - World Champ is a Punch-Out!! alternative arriving just as the Punch-Out!! mascot lost his real-life star.
  - Does a stat-building boxer feel more like "real boxing" than Punch-Out!!'s pattern puzzles? Fans in 1991 could argue that.
  - Famous names sold boxing games in 1990 (Tyson, then Douglas). World Champ had no licensed star, so it had to sell on gameplay alone.
  - No NES tie-in game for Rocky V turned up.

## VI. Reception & Legacy

- It is a reasonably obscure Romstar release. Collector price listings put it around $40 (Rarity Club average, about $41.62), so it is not expensive, but it is not common either.
- The Japanese version's thinly veiled real boxers make it more interesting than the Western release, which is a small tragedy of localization.

## VII. Sequels and Spinoffs

- The name "Great Boxing" hints at a Japanese lineage, but no series connection was verified, so do not claim one on the show.
- Comparison games
  - Ring King: the closest relative, with stat building and a ladder of opponents. Ring King is more arcade-style, and World Champ adds the training matches.
  - Punch-Out!!: the obvious benchmark. Pattern puzzle vs. stat management.
  - Heavyweight Championship Boxing (Game Boy): the same year, the same point-distribution idea.

## VIII. Essential Games List Vote

- Case for Essential
  - It does something most NES boxers do not: train a character, then fight with the build you made.
  - Controls are responsive and the punch-direction scheme is easy to learn.
  - The 8-player tournament mode is a good group-play hook.
  - It sits right behind Punch-Out!! on a short list of good NES boxing games.
- Case against Essential
  - It leans on Ring King's template rather than inventing something new.
  - Once your stats are high, fights can become button-mashing.
  - The shrill referee voice is a real mark against it.
  - The Western version lost the Japanese version's famous-boxer parodies, which was most of the personality.
  - It is a fine game, but not one every NES player has to experience.
