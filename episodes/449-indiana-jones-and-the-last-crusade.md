# 449 - Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade

- Opening hook: STEP ON IT JUNIOR

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: the movie chopped into six set pieces, and you pick the order
    - A package from Venice turns out to be Henry Jones Sr.'s diary, and a telegram from Marcus Brody says the Cross of Coronado is sitting on a ship off Portugal.
  - From there the game hands you a menu of destinations and lets you decide where Indy goes next.
- Software Creations developed it, Taito America published it, March 1991
  - Software Creations was the Manchester studio behind Solstice, Silver Surfer, and Pictionary.
  - Michael Crick is credited as designer, artist, and co-programmer, which tells you roughly how big this team was.
- The identity problem: three Last Crusade games exist, and two of them are on the NES
  - LucasArts made the point-and-click Graphic Adventure in 1989.
  - US Gold and Tiertex made The Action Game the same year, which NMS Software later ported to the NES for Ubisoft in 1993.
  - This one is the outlier: NES exclusive, built from scratch, no computer original behind it.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- You choose the order, and the order sets the difficulty
  - Every stage gets harder the more stages you have already cleared.
  - The Cross of Coronado asks for 15 enemies before the boss appears if you take it first, and 30 tougher ones if you save it (GameFAQs).
  - Two stages, the Cross and Venice, fall off the menu permanently if you ignore them twice.
- You can skip most of the game if you want to
  - Play the bare minimum, head straight to the temple, take the lesser ending.
  - Clear everything and you get the best ending.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Six scenes with cutscenes between them
  - The Cross of Coronado (ship), Venice (sliding puzzle), Castle Brunwald (maze), Iskenderun (tank), the Road to Berlin (motorcycle), and the Lost Temple.
- The combat loop
  - You never face more than one enemy at a time, which is the most generous design decision in the game.
  - The whip is a one-hit kill at the right distance. Fists are close to useless.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- Lose a level and the Nazis take Dad's diary, and getting it back is the punishment
  - Fail almost anything and the diary is gone. Fail the Cross of Coronado and it is a straight game over.
  - The Road to Berlin motorcycle stage is how you win it back, and it is the hardest thing in the game.
  - Skip the recovery and the finale gets much worse, because the diary carries the symbol you need at the end.
- Castle Brunwald is a maze in three dimensions
  - Five layers deep, three floors high, and you only ever see a slice of it.
  - Notches on the floor and shields on the wall are your only orientation cues.
  - Whip the torches to open hidden passages. Sit in the chairs to open the fireplaces.
  - On harder settings doors lock and the route through changes completely.
- Venice is a sliding puzzle with a fire timer
  - Reassemble a sketch of the Grail while flames climb up the bottom of the screen.
  - You survive either way, but bail early and you only carry out part of the picture.
- The ending is an actual test, not a boss
  - Walk the floor spelling JEHOVAH one tile at a time while your torch burns down. Wrong letter, you fall.
  - Then step off the correct symbol into open air.
  - Then pick the true Grail out of a lineup, checking lip, cup shape, handles, stem, and base against the sketch from Venice.
  - Choose poorly and you lose the entire run at the last possible second.

## V. Music & Presentation

- Tim and Geoff Follin wrote the score
  - Same brothers, same studio, same era as Silver Surfer and Solstice, which is the whole pitch for taking this soundtrack seriously.
  - The John Williams theme is in here, and it is the thing most people remember hearing.
- Digitized actors on an NES cart
  - Cutscenes use sepia-toned bitmapped photos of Harrison Ford, Sean Connery, and Alison Doody.
  - Worth pausing on how rare an actual actor likeness was on the NES in 1991.

## VII. Sequels, Spinoffs, and Comparison Games

- There is no sequel to this game
- The early 90s wave (relevant to the show):
- Indiana Jones and the Fate of Atlantis (1992, LucasArts) and a separate Fate of Atlantis: The Action Game the same year, repeating the two-games-one-title pattern from Last Crusade
- The Young Indiana Jones Chronicles (Jaleco, NES, December 1992), based on the TV series Wikipedia
- Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade (Ubisoft/NMS, NES and Game Boy, 1993), the port of the 1989 Action Game
- Instruments of Chaos starring Young Indiana Jones (1994, Sega Genesis) Fandom
- Indiana Jones' Greatest Adventures (1994, SNES), which covers all three original films on the Super Star Wars engine
- The LucasArts years:
- Indiana Jones and His Desktop Adventures (1996), Indiana Jones and the Infernal Machine (1999, PC and N64), and a separate 2D Infernal Machine for Game Boy Color in 2001 Wikipedia
- Indiana Jones and the Emperor's Tomb (2003), generally considered the best of the 3D ones Den of Geek
- Three Adventures of Young Indiana Jones edutainment PC titles in 2007 and 2008
- The Crystal Skull era:
- LEGO Indiana Jones: The Original Adventures (2008) and LEGO Indiana Jones 2 (2009)
- Indiana Jones and the Staff of Kings (2009, Wii/PS2/PSP/DS)
- A mobile Kingdom of the Crystal Skull tie-in (2008)
- Then a fifteen-year gap, and:
- Indiana Jones and the Great Circle (MachineGames/Bethesda), Xbox and PC in December 2024, PS5 on April 27, 2025, and Switch 2 on May 12, 2026. Its story DLC, The Order of Giants, landed September 4, 2025.
- Comparison games
  - Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom (NES, 1988): the earlier Indy attempt on the system, and the bar this one clears without much effort.
  - The Adventures of Bayou Billy: another license that mixes brawling with vehicle stages and lives or dies on how the punching feels.

## VIII. Essential Games List Vote

- Case for Essential
  - The single strongest argument: the level select is a real design idea, not a menu. You choose the order, the order sets the difficulty, and two stages walk away if you ignore them. Almost nothing else in the 1991 licensed-game pile was thinking that hard.
  - Supporting: a Follin score and digitized movie stars make it a genuine showpiece for what the NES could look and sound like.
- Case against Essential
  - The single strongest argument: the thing you spend most of the game doing, hitting one guy at a time, feels unreliable, and no amount of clever structure fixes an action game whose action you cannot trust.
  - Supporting: the finale can erase an entire clean run on a single guess about the shape of a cup handle.
