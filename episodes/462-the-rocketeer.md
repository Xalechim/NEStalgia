# 462 - The Rocketeer

- "There was only one course of action: he must become THE ROCKETEER!"

&nbsp;

## I. Setup & Premise

- What it is based on: Disney's 1991 film The Rocketeer, which itself came from Dave Stevens' comic
  - The film opened June 21, 1991. The cartridge hit shelves in May 1991, about seven weeks before the movie, so you could play the adaptation before you saw the film. Like the Spider-Man game on GC/PS2/XBOX
  - It is set in Los Angeles in 1938. Stunt pilot Cliff Secord finds a stolen experimental rocket pack hidden in his plane. Gangsters, G-men, the Army and Nazi agents all want it.
  - Movie star Neville Sinclair kidnaps Cliff's girlfriend, Jenny Blake, and offers to trade her for the pack. That is the box story, and it ends with Cliff deciding he has to become the Rocketeer.
- Who made it
  - Realtime Associates developed it, with Ironwind Software also credited, and Bandai published it in North America in May 1991. (Wikipedia, LaunchBox)
  - Producer and director was David Warhol, the founder of Realtime.
  - Wikipedia lists a North American release only.
- A quick correction for anyone who remembers a different Rocketeer game
  - The PC and Super NES games were made by NovaLogic, and the PC one was published by Walt Disney Computer Software. They are completely different games with different designs. This episode is the NES platformer from Bandai.
- The pitch
  - A side-scrolling run-and-gun platformer with a jet pack you have to fuel. Six chapters, cutscenes that retell the film, and a showdown with Sinclair at the end.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- You start the game with nothing but your fists
  - There are six weapons: fist, pistol, tommy gun, spread gun, grenade and bazooka. They share one ammo pool, and the more powerful the weapon, the more ammo each shot costs.
    - What do you think of universal ammo versus specific ammo?
  - It runs from one bullet for the pistol up to fifteen for the bazooka. The fist costs nothing, and it stuns most basic enemies, so a lot of players just punch.
- The jet pack is a pickup, not a power. Surprising?
  - Fuel cans give you a fuel meter that runs up to eleven bars. Flight is fast and a bit floaty, and you can fly in any direction.
  - It is only really usable in certain stages, and some boss fights. Everywhere else the game blocks it.
- Hearts and belts from fallen enemies
  - Health goes up to eight slots. Pink hearts add one, and rarer purple hearts add more. Ammo belts add ten shots. Most pickups drop from enemies, and not every enemy drops anything.
- Cutscenes that tell the movie
  - Between chapters you get illustrated story scenes that follow the film. The best looking thing in the game.
- Passwords and one life
  - You get a nine digit password between chapters, and continues are unlimited. Cliff has a single life bar, and each death comes with a short sequence of stills.
- High Score Board
  - In the opening shot the Hollywood sign appears on screen, and the in-game text calls it just Hollywood. In 1938 the real sign read HOLLYWOODLAND. The LAND letters were taken down in September 1949, so for the year the game is set in, the sign is wrong.
  - Fans guess it is a licensing issue. The Hollywood Chamber of Commerce does charge fees for commercial use of the sign, but nobody has said that is why. It is a strange mistake in a game that works hard on 1938 details.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- The core loop
  - Walk or fly through a long stage, punch or shoot what the gangsters throw at you, and grab fuel, hearts and ammo from what they drop. At the end of a chapter you get a cutscene and a password.
  - Six chapters. Some have sublevels, and four of them end in a boss. Two bosses are big fights where you get all your weapons, and two are smaller ones with limited options.
  - Health, ammo and fuel do not refill between stages, so you carry whatever you have left into the next one. Continuing costs you all your ammo, but you restart from the latest screen transition.
- Controls
  - D-pad: walk left and right, and Down to duck.
  - A: jump. Press A again near the top of the jump to switch the jet pack on. Hold Down and press A to drop out of a jump early.
  - B: attack with the weapon you have selected.
  - Select: cycle weapons.
  - Start: pause, and skip cutscenes.
  - Reviewers say you cannot cycle weapons while paused, so you have to switch in the middle of the action.
- Enemies
  - Most gray-suited goons take three pistol shots. Doors keep producing more of them, and some enemies spawn right on top of you.
  - Runners kill you in one hit, and the mobsters get faster as the game goes on.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- The jet pack does not follow you from stage to stage
  - You can use it in the forest and in some boss fights, but a hangar roof or a tree branch will catch you if you steer badly. Flight comes in sudden bursts, so dodging bullets in the air is awkward.
- Weapons that are right for some enemies and wrong for others
  - A grenade can sail over the next goon's head and leave you open to a punch, and the bazooka is best saved for the bosses.
- The long mazes
  - The last two chapters are the ones people complain about. One reviewer found the fifth chapter harder than the final one, and the finale has a sharp jump in difficulty and confusing layouts.
- The debug code
  - Enter the password 169-963-491 and you unlock a cheat mode. After that, Select plus Start skips the current stage, and holding A while you press them refills your health, ammo and fuel. (Cutting Room Floor, GameFAQs)
- How fast it can be beaten
  - The tool-assisted record is 13 minutes 55 seconds. It uses double shots by firing just before you land, starts the next stage at zero health by finishing before the death animation, and cancels the jet pack between fuel drops. It also barely uses the jet pack. (TASVideos)

## V. History and Development

- Realtime Associates
  - Based in El Segundo, California. David Warhol and a group of former Mattel Electronics employees founded it in 1986, and the first work was Intellivision games.
  - Its other NES work includes Caesars Palace, Dick Tracy, Fun House, Maniac Mansion and Monster Truck Rally. (Wikipedia)
  - So this was a studio used to doing licensed work for several publishers, not a Disney in-house team.
- The credits
  - George Sanger, credited as "The Fatman," and Dave Hayes did the music, and David Warhol produced and directed. Warhol arranged the music and wrote the NES sound driver. (VGMPF)
- The license
  - Bandai America published it, while Disney's own computer software label put out the separate PC game.
  - The NES game follows the film's story rather than Stevens' comic.
- The game that was never made
  - A former Disney Interactive concept artist, Ken Christiansen, pitched a Rocketeer game in an internal Disney contest. It was a World War II flight game that mixed airplane combat with stealing vehicles in the style of Grand Theft Auto. Disney thought it was too expensive and had no marketing hook, such as a new film or show. (Time Extension)

## VI. Music and Presentation

- Graphics
  - The 1938 setting is well chosen, with planes in the hangars, gangsters in suits and pulp-era props. The NES palette just cannot hold it.
- Music
  - There are twelve tracks: title, chapter start, four cutscene themes, two stage themes, a boss track, a death jingle, a stage clear and the ending.
  - The stage music is fast rock, and the cutscene themes are more orchestral. The title theme is built on the movie's main theme.
  - Sanger later scored the Super NES game, and its music reuses ideas from the NES ending theme.

## VII. Pop Culture

- The film
  - Joe Johnston directed it. Danny Bilson and Paul De Meo wrote it. Billy Campbell played Cliff, Jennifer Connelly was Jenny, Timothy Dalton was Sinclair, a villain modeled on Errol Flynn, Alan Arkin was Peevy, Paul Sorvino was Eddie Valentine and Terry O'Quinn was Howard Hughes. (Wikipedia)
  - The budget was between 35 and 40 million dollars. It opened with 9.6 million dollars in fourth place, behind Robin Hood: Prince of Thieves, City Slickers and Dying Young, and made about 46.6 million dollars in the United States.
  - Critics were kinder than the box office: 73 percent on Rotten Tomatoes, and an A minus from audiences.
  - James Horner scored it. Disney had talked about sequels, but those plans stopped in July 1991.
  - Merchandise: Pizza Hut and M&M's ran tie-ins.
    - Get A Rocketeer Kids Pizza Pack From Pizza Hult
    - One-topping Personal Pan Pizza®
    - Unique Rocketeer Cup with Helmet
    - Rocketeer Glider (paper airplane)
- The comic
  - Dave Stevens created the Rocketeer after Pacific Comics approached him in 1981. It debuted as a back-up feature in Starslayer #2 in 1982.
  - Betty, Cliff's girlfriend in the comic, was drawn from the pinup model Bettie Page. Stevens says he later became friends with her.
  - His influences include Commando Cody from the Republic serials and Doc Savage. In the comic the rocket pack's inventor is never named, and in the film he became Howard Hughes.
  - Pacific folded in 1984, so Eclipse finished the story. Later it went to Comico, then Dark Horse in 1995, and IDW from 2009, after Stevens died in 2008.
- Disney after the film
  - The Rocketeer came back as a Disney Junior cartoon in November 2019, starring Cliff's great-granddaughter Kit Secord. A reboot was announced in 2016, and The Return of the Rocketeer in 2021, but neither film was made.
- The period
  - The film arrived in a crowded June: Robin Hood: Prince of Thieves and City Slickers both beat it on its opening weekend.

## VIII. Sequels, Spinoffs and Comparison Games

- There was no NES sequel. These are the other Rocketeer games and relatives.
- The Rocketeer (MS-DOS, 1992)
  - It supports the Disney Sound Source, an external audio box for digitized sound.
  - It mixes airplane and jet pack races with fast combat against enemy soldiers. COMPUTE! gave it a strong recommendation in September 1992, with the complaints that it is hard and has no save feature.
- The Rocketeer (Super NES, 1992)
  - It is a collection of mini-games: three biplane races, two shooting galleries, two flying shooter stages and a zeppelin fight at the end.
  - A full playthrough takes about 24 minutes. One reviewer found it badly designed and said never to play it, and the zeppelin fight is compared to Urban Champion.
- Modern comparison games
  - Jetpack Joyride (Halfbrick Studios, September 1, 2011, iOS, later Android, PlayStation and more)
    - A one-touch endless runner where Barry Steakfries steals an experimental jet pack. It shares the jet pack hero who gets the device by stealing or finding it.
    - Where the Rocketeer makes you manage fuel and walk through stages, Jetpack Joyride lets you fly constantly, and you hold to rise and let go to fall.
    - A moment to point at: the rainbow boxes that give you a temporary vehicle, which the NES game has nothing like.
  - Rocket Knight (Climax Studios, Konami, May 2010, Windows, Xbox 360, PlayStation 3)
    - A revival of Sparkster, a hero with a sword and a rocket pack, in a side-scrolling platformer with shooting sections. It is a fair comparison because both are about a rocket pack that moves a platformer hero in bursts.
    - The difference: Sparkster uses the pack as a launch and ricochet move that you aim at enemies, while Cliff uses fuel to fly.
    - A moment to point at: the burst vortex that sends enemy bombs back at them.
  - Crimson Skies: High Road to Revenge (FASA Studio, Microsoft Game Studios, October 21, 2003, Xbox)
    - An arcade flight game set in an alternate 1930s America full of air pirates and zeppelins. It has the same pulp-era look as the film.
    - You fly planes, not a jet pack. Takeoffs and landings are automatic, and you can steal other planes.
    - A moment to point at: plundering a neutral airship, and your own zeppelin base, the Pandora.
  - Cuphead (Studio MDHR, September 29, 2017, Windows and Xbox One, later Switch and PlayStation 4)
    - A run-and-gun game styled after 1930s cartoons that mixes platforming with boss fights and some airplane shooter stages. The Rocketeer is also a 1930s-themed run-and-gun game with boss fights.
    - The difference is polish. Every frame of Cuphead is hand drawn, and the soundtrack is big band jazz. The NES palette cannot compete with that.
    - A moment to point at: the plane boss stages, and the pink objects you can parry.

## IX. Essential Games List

- Case for Essential
  - It is a rare licensed game that keeps the shape of its movie, with real chapter structure and passwords.
  - The fuel meter and flight give it a hook that most NES platformers did not have, and loose copies are cheap, around ten to twenty dollars.
- Case against Essential
  - The jet pack is the whole reason to play, and the game keeps taking it away. You spend most of it walking.
  - It has one life bar and spawns enemies on top of you. HonestGamers scored it 2 out of 5, calling it a forgettable bargain-bin title.
- Suggested verdict: not essential. It is a decent curiosity for people who love the movie, with reviews ranging from 2 out of 5 to 7.5 out of 10.
