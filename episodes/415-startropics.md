# 415 - StarTropics

- Tropical Adventure of Epic Dimensions
- 1. General Overview
- What it is
 StarTropics is Nintendo’s 1990 top-down action-adventure for NES, following a “towns plus dungeons” structure that often gets compared to Zelda, but with its own tropical, pulp-adventure vibe. Wikipedia+1
- Who made it
 Developed by Nintendo R&D3 and Locomotive Corporation, published by Nintendo. Produced, written, and directed by Genyo Takeda. Wikipedia
- Release footprint
 Not released in Japan. Released in North America (1990) and later in Europe. Wikipedia+1
- Core identity
 Exploration, puzzle-solving, and action combat with a signature main weapon: a yo-yo
- Offbeat questions
- Does StarTropics feel like “Nintendo does Zelda in the tropics,” or like a different vibe just wearing Zelda’s clothes? Wikipedia
- Is the yo-yo as iconic as Link’s sword, or does it feel like a placeholder until you get “real weapons”? Wikipedia
- 2. Story and Objective
- Premise
 You play as Mike Jones, a teenager who travels to a tropical archipelago to visit his archaeologist uncle, Dr. Steven Jones, and quickly gets pulled into a rescue mission. Wikipedia

- 3. World Structure and Core Loop
- Overworld exploration
 You move through island towns and outdoor areas, talk to NPCs, and use items to unlock new routes. Wikipedia+1
- Dungeon progression
 Each major area tends to funnel you into a dungeon or setpiece where you solve puzzles, collect key items, and beat a boss to move forward. Wikipedia
- Island hopping and vehicles
 The game explicitly mixes travel on foot with travel using your uncle’s submarine, the Sub-C (with assistant ROB), which creates a “chaptered adventure” feel. Nintendo+1

 Mike’s movement is more “action game” than “RPG,” and the way he jumps and collides with enemies is part of the learning curve (especially early). Nintendo World Report+1

- 5. Items, Tools, and Puzzle Language
- Key items as progression gates
 StarTropics is built on “get an item, recontextualize the world,” with tools that let you cross hazards, access new zones, and solve dungeon-specific puzzles. Wikipedia
- Real examples of “key items as progression gates”
- StarTropics loves giving you an item that changes what “counts” as a wall, hazard, or enemy.
- Bottle note + code (Chapter 2)
  - Before: black “wave” tiles are just blocked water.
  - After: you activate Sub-C’s submergible system and can press B on those waves to dive through rock barriers and reach the next cave. tartarus.rpgclassics.com+1
- Crystal Ball (Chapter 3)
  - Before: the fortune teller is a dead end, and SheCola is effectively locked.
  - After: retrieving the Crystal Ball triggers the chain that gets you into SheCola (including the transformation workaround), which then leads to your Shooting Star upgrade and the path to the Hermit. tartarus.rpgclassics.com+1
- Rod of Sight (Ghost content)
  - Before: you can take damage from “nothing” and can’t fight back.
  - After: Rod of Sight reveals invisible ghosts (the enemies page even calls out that you can’t see them without it). 任天堂ホームページ+1
- Captain Bell’s memorial music clue (Chapter 5)
  - So America is AmericaCola but Britian is just Britian? Captain Bell is british.
  - Before: flames block the exit path.
  - After: you interpret the parrot’s “Do me so far, do me” hint as literal solfege, play the organ sequence, extinguish the flames, and only then can you reach the dungeon that clears the strait. tartarus.rpgclassics.com
- The letter-in-water frequency (Chapter 4)
  - Before: you don’t have the tracking frequency needed when Nav-Com asks for it.
  - After: the water-revealed frequency becomes the “real-world” gate that unlocks the next step. tartarus.rpgclassics.com+1
- Magic Cubes (Chapters 7–8)
  - They are literal progression keys inside the alien ship: collecting them upgrades your gear and is required to resolve the story and reveal the Argonian survivors.
- NPC hints matter
 The game expects you to talk to people and interpret directions. It is designed around curiosity and note-taking.

- 6. Dungeons, Bosses, and Setpiece Variety
- Dungeon identity
 Dungeons are typically thematically tied to an island or region, with unique hazards and puzzle gimmicks. Wikipedia
- How dungeons work in StarTropics
- The game has two modes:
  - Travel stage: overworld islands and Sub-C navigation. You talk to NPCs and move between islands. 任天堂ホームページ
  - Battle stage: triggers when you enter tunnels/caves (the dungeon spaces). You fight, solve room hazards, and manage weapons/items from the pause menu. 任天堂ホームページ
- Dungeons are multi-room, top-down labyrinths with:
  - Enemy rooms mixed with hazard rooms (spike traps, moving dangers, “can’t be destroyed” obstacles).
  - Puzzle “reads” that often involve fake walls, hidden entrances, or using the correct magic item for that dungeon’s gimmick (Ghost content is the cleanest example). tartarus.rpgclassics.com+1
- The game frequently scores you after finishing a dungeon, reinforcing the idea that each one is a contained challenge run. tartarus.rpgclassics.com
- Structure-wise, the later-game dungeons escalate into themed “sectors” (the spaceship is literally broken into Sector 1, Sector 2, then the Control Center).
- Boss structure
 Boss fights act as chapter caps, often requiring you to recognize patterns and use the tools you have recently obtained.
- Enemy design
 The enemy mix leans into “tropical weird plus sci-fi weird,” rather than pure fantasy.
- Tropical weird
- Dodo (yes, extinct dodos) rushing you down. StarTropics
- Octot hopping around wet regions. StarTropics
- Looper snakes in multiple behaviors/colors. StarTropics
- Mud-o-fish / Puff style water ambushers. StarTropics
- Undead and “island ghost story” weird
- Muumu (mummies) and Bonehead / Bonedog. StarTropics
- Minie (invisible ghosts) that only become fair fights once you use Rod of Sight. StarTropics+1
- Pirate Ghoul that’s best handled via reflected projectiles (mirror logic). StarTropics+1
- Sci-fi weird
- Death Discs (spiky “keep coming” hazards with stronger gold variants). StarTropics
- Electricity hazards that chase/pattern around you. StarTropics
- Mobilized Turrets and Space Troopers that feel like you suddenly walked into a different genre once the spaceship starts.
- 9. The Famous “Dip the Letter in Water” Moment
- What happened on original NES carts
 The NES release came with a physical letter from your uncle. At one point the game requires a radio frequency, and players were expected to dip the letter in water to reveal the hidden code. Wikipedia+2Nintendo+2
- The code
 The frequency is 747. Nintendo Support still publishes this directly. Nintendo Support
- In response to questions from fans, the code was also published in Nintendo Power.
- Re-release handling
 Virtual Console versions reproduced the letter in the electronic manual. Switch Online initially confused some players because the physical-letter trick doesn’t happen, so Nintendo also published the code online. Wikipedia+2Nintendo Support+2

- Island towns and the main story hook in each
- Chapter 1
- CoralCola (C-Island)
  - Hook: Your uncle (Dr. Jones) has been kidnapped, and the Chief points you toward the island’s “test of courage” to begin the rescue. You get your first weapon and head into your first tunnel dungeon. tartarus.rpgclassics.com
- Chapter 2
- No named “Cola” town here (lighthouse and a seaside hut area)
  - Hook: A dolphin begs you to find her missing son. While searching, you find a bottle with a note from your uncle saying he was abducted by aliens, and you activate Sub-C’s ability to submerge. tartarus.rpgclassics.com+1
- Chapter 3
- MiraCola
  - Hook: The Sub-C is wrecked, and MiraCola offers repairs if you solve their crisis: the chief’s daughter Bananette is in a deep magical sleep, so you have to seek a cure. tartarus.rpgclassics.com
- SheCola
  - Hook: You need help reaching the Mountain Hermit, but SheCola is gated (only women can enter). You end up restoring the fortune teller’s Crystal Ball and get transformed so you can enter and receive a major weapon upgrade. tartarus.rpgclassics.com
- Ghost Village
  - Hook: You drain the pond to retrieve the Crystal Ball, which is the key that unlocks the SheCola path forward. tartarus.rpgclassics.com
- Chapter 4
- TunaCola
  - Hook: You chase leads, get swallowed by a giant whale, reunite with Baboo, and retrieve a lighter to escape. This chapter also tees up the famous “dip the letter in water” frequency code setup. tartarus.rpgclassics.com+1
- Chapter 5
- BellCola
  - Hook: The strait is blocked by a ship. BellCola’s chief points you to Captain Bell’s legacy, you solve the memorial organ “Do me so far, do me” puzzle, then clear Captain Bell’s cave so the ship sinks and the route opens. tartarus.rpgclassics.com
- Chapter 6
- Howduyadu-Cola
  - Hook: You’re navigating by coordinates and hidden passages, then you finally reunite with your uncle. He explains the Argonia backstory and that the aliens took the three Magic Cubes, setting up the assault on the alien ship. tartarus.rpgclassics.com
- Chapter 7
- Zoda’s spaceship
  - Hook: You board the ship, clear Sector dungeons, and collect Magic Cubes. The first cube upgrades your main weapon into Super Nova, and the second cube boosts your max hearts. tartarus.rpgclassics.com+1
- Chapter 8
- Zoda’s spaceship and then back to CoralCola
  - Hook: You confront Zoda, get the final cube, escape the exploding ship, and return to C-Island where the cubes reveal the Argonian survivors.
