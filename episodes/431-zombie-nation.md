# 431 - Zombie Nation

- 1999. What appeared to be a harmless meteorite crashing in the Nevada desert had turned out to be Darc Seed, an evil alien creature with horrible powers.

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: A meteorite crashes in the Nevada desert. It's not a meteorite — it's an alien called Darc Seed. Using "magnetic rays," Darc Seed zombifies the entire United States, brings the Statue of Liberty to life (there’s now a collection of NES games where the statue of liberty comes to life), and seizes a legendary samurai sword called Shura.
- The only force capable of stopping this is a giant, levitating, disembodied samurai head named Namakubi — which translates literally to "severed head" — who crosses the Pacific to save America by shooting eyeballs and vomit at buildings
- That is the actual plot. That is the game you are playing
- Developed by KAZe
- headed by Junichiro Kawazoe, a former company director at record firm and game publisher Meldac.
- The head of development was Norio Nakagata, previously a composer at Namco.
- The studio was literally founded the month before the Japanese release.
- The premise is absurd, the execution commits fully to the absurdity.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The protagonist holds up
- You are a head. Not a character with a head. Not a character who lost their head. A head. A giant, floating, orange samurai head drifting through the skies above American cities, firing eyeballs from its eyes and vomiting projectiles from its mouth.
- Your health bar is displayed as a row of eight miniature Namakubi heads at the bottom of the screen.
- When Namakubi gets hit, one head turns into a skull. If all heads become skulls, you're dead
- There is a lore justification: Namakubi is the great head of a samurai warrior whose spirit crossed the ocean to reclaim the sword Shura from Darc Seed.
- This is apparently a connection to the historical figure Taira no Masakado, a real samurai whose decapitated head is said to have become a vengeful spirit, searching for its body after it was buried separately.
- The Japanese version: Abarenbō Tengu context
- The Japanese version replaces the samurai head with a giant floating tengu mask from Japanese folklore.
- Tengu are long-nosed supernatural beings from Japanese mythology — mountain spirits associated with martial arts, mischief, and divine retribution.
- In Japan, the game reads as a cheeky yokai action game. For the American release, the tengu mask was swapped for a samurai head, the backstory was rewritten around zombies and aliens, and the title became Zombie Nation
- The localization team correctly identified that an American audience would have no frame of reference for a tengu and went with the next-closest thing. Whether "giant severed samurai head" is actually more accessible than "giant demon mask" is debatable, but the intent was a cultural one.
- The localization didn't stop at the protagonist; they also overhauled The Statue of Liberty's crown. It was transformed into a writhing tangle of snakes, evoking a Medusa aesthetic, while her palette was adjusted to more closely mirror her real-world appearance.
- The shoot-'em-up mechanics mixed
- It's a side-scrolling shmup with a twist: If you rescue a falling zombie hostage it slowly powers up your attacks.  You blast buildings apart — everything is destructible, skyscrapers crumble, smoke stacks collapse — and zombified civilians leap from the wreckage.
- You can fly around somewhat lazily and take a substantial amount of punishment. The player can regenerate some of their life by defeating enemies and structures. Which creates a loop where aggression is the best path forward. The more you destroy, the more health you recover. The game wants you to blow everything up, and it rewards you for doing so
- Your hitbox is enormous — you ARE a giant head — and in crowded sections, threading through projectile patterns with a sprite that large becomes genuinely demanding. It's the one place where the absurd premise creates real mechanical consequences
- The stage select holds up
- The game allows the player, like in the Mega Man series, to select any stage at will. Once a stage is selected, the following stages play sequentially, cycling from stage 4 back to 1.
- The bosses holds up
- The Statue of Liberty is Stage 1 — snake-haired, torch-wielding, spitting bullets at you while you shoot eyeballs at her face.
- Zombified Paul Bunyan and his axes
- A battleship called Atlas.
- The final boss, Darc Seed himself, is anticlimactic
- The Konami Code fun fact
- Inputting the Konami Code during gameplay refills your life bar. A Konami cheat code in a non-Konami game. Nobody knows why it's there. It just is

## III. Pop Culture, Legacy & Connections

- The collector's market:
- Current loose cartridge prices sit around $150, complete in box around $335. It's not Stadium Events money.
- Meldac and KAZe's trajectory: KAZe went on to become pinball and pachinko game specialists (maybe that’s why the Konami code is in the game),
- The 2021 re-release: City Connection released Abarenbō Tengu & Zombie Nation for Nintendo Switch and PC on October 28, 2021 bundling both the Japanese and American versions together for the first time.
- The "Destructive Savior" problem:
- Namakubi saves the U.S. by shooting his own eyeballs and vomit at zombified American citizens, eating their corpses, and destroying random buildings along the way.
- This is like a reverse Godzilla situation where a Japanese entity saves America from itself by obliterating its infrastructure and eating its citizens.
- Thematic relatives:
- Parodius (Konami, 1988+) — the closest spiritual cousin. Both take the shmup framework and inject it with aggressive absurdity, though Parodius does it through Konami self-parody
- Cho Aniki (1992, TurboGrafx) — both games share a commitment to the grotesque that most Japanese developers wouldn't touch. Zombie Nation got there first

## IV. Closing Bits

- In a medium that now celebrates indie weirdness — Katamari Damacy and Undertale to name two — was Zombie Nation thirty years ahead of its audience?
