# 418 - TMNT II: The Arcade Game

- You may have Shredder double-teamed, but he still has you outnumbered
- 1. General Overview
- What it is
 TMNT II: The Arcade Game is a home conversion of Konami’s 1989 TMNT arcade beat em up.
- Release and labeling
 Released on NES in December 1990, and published in North America under Konami’s Ultra Games label
- Konami used the Ultra Games label on NES as a workaround for Nintendo of America’s licensing rules.
- Nintendo capped output per publisher. Under Nintendo’s standard NES license, each licensee was limited to five new NES games per year (and there were other tight controls, like exclusivity windows). This “five games” limit is explicitly described in the Atari v. Nintendo court decision when it summarizes Nintendo’s license terms. digital-law-online.info
- Konami wanted to release more than five games. So Konami created a separate U.S. subsidiary/shell publisher, Ultra Software Corporation (Ultra Games), and published some NES titles under that label to effectively get an additional allotment of releases. Wikipedia+1
- It went away once it stopped being useful. As Nintendo loosened restrictions around the early SNES era, Ultra Games lost its purpose and Konami phased it out (the label is generally cited as ending around 1992).
- 2. TMNT as a franchise in 1990
- Peak visibility moment
 The live-action Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles (1990) film hit theaters March 30, 1990 and became a huge box office success, crossing roughly $200M worldwide. Wikipedia
- Cartoon was still cranking
 The 1987 animated series rolled into Season 4 starting in 1990, expanded to 41 episodes, and even shifted partway into CBS Saturday morning programming. Wikipedia
- Merch machine at full blast
 A Los Angeles Times business piece later described the Turtle fad as peaking in 1990, citing it as an enormous share of the action figure market at the time. Los Angeles Times
- Video game ecosystem
 Konami already had the 1989 arcade brawler out in the world, and the NES port arrives right in the middle of “Turtles are everywhere.” Wikipedia

- 4. Core Gameplay Loop
- Moment to moment
  - Walk right, beat Foot soldiers and themed enemies
  - Use jump attacks to control space
  - Grab pizza to heal
  - Hit a boss gate, learn the pattern, repeat GameFAQs+1
- Modes
  - 1-player
  - 2-player co-op (simultaneous) GameFAQs+1
- Why it works
 It is built around satisfying crowd control and readable enemy “tells”
- Team personality quick hits (NES)
- Donatello
 Longest weapon range (staff), great for controlling crowds from a safe distance. The tradeoff is his normal swing is the slowest, so whiffs feel expensive. GameFAQs
- Michelangelo
 Second-longest reach (nunchucks) with a swing that feels “wide” and forgiving in scrums. Still slower than Leo and Raph, so he plays best when you are already in control of the screen. GameFAQs
- Raphael
 Shortest reach (sai), but fastest normal attack, so he is the most aggressive and best at rapid clean-ups once you are in. You will rely more on jump-kicks and positioning to avoid getting stuffed at range. GameFAQs
- Leonardo
 Second-shortest reach (sword) and second-fastest swing, so he feels like the “steady middle.” If you do not want to think about matchups, Leo is the simplest “always fine” pick.
- 5. Controls and Combat Feel
- Basic controls (NES)
  - A: Jump (also used to confirm turtle select)
  - B: Attack
  - A + B: Jumping kick GameFAQs
- Combat language
  - You win by spacing, juggling knockdowns, and not getting surrounded.
  - Enemies often enter in waves that punish standing still.
- Character feel
 The NES version is generally discussed as more streamlined than the arcade, with a simpler, more consistent combat rhythm. Old School Gamer Magazine
- Offbeat questions
- Is the “jumping kick” the real main weapon, with the normal attack as backup? GameFAQs

- 9. Differences between the NES version and the 1989 arcade game
- This is the big “compare and contrast” section for your episode.
- Big structural differences
- Players
  - Arcade can be up to 4 players
  - NES is up to 2 players Old School Gamer Magazine+1
- Content additions on NES
 The NES port adds two new levels and two new bosses created for the port, Tora and Shogun (not TMNT canon). Wikipedia+2Nintendo World Report+2
- Stage expansion and boss swaps
Several arcade stages were extended on NES, and a key swap: NES uses Baxter’s mutated fly form in that spot instead of the arcade’s Bebop and Rocksteady pairing). Wikipedia
- Feel and “arcade-ness”
- Arcade presentation
 More voice clips and dialogue, more spectacle. Nintendo Fandom
- NES gameplay adjustments
 Often described as more streamlined, less combo-centric, and more consistent in how many hits enemies take. Old School Gamer Magazine
- Balance and recovery
- Health behavior
 One documented change: NES refills health after boss fights, while arcade does not do that in the same way for several bosses. Nintendo Fandom
- Small but fun “port fingerprints”
- The NES port era also includes notable Pizza Hut product placement in connection with the game’s marketing and materials. Wikipedia
- 1) PIZZA HUT - The box callout: “This game comes with pizza”
- Early print runs straight-up advertise the deal on the retail packaging. The box copy calls out a free Personal Pan Pizza coupon inside and even includes regional fine print like void in Quebec and Mexico (plus the classic “Not Sponsored by Nintendo”). Nintendo Player
- Why that matters for the era:
- This is pre-internet hype. The box is the billboard.
- For kids, “new TMNT game” plus “pizza tonight” is basically unbeatable leverage.
- Collector angle:
- There are later “no pizza offer” variants where the front-of-box blurb is removed. PriceCharting
- Heritage Auctions has even singled out “with Pizza Offer” as a notable feature of early production copies. Heritage Auctions
- 2) The manual coupon: a literal tear-off “quest item”
- The coupon itself is on the back of the instruction manual, meaning you had to either:
- cut it out, or
- tear the manual (a tragedy you only understand years later).
- Key specifics that are great to say out loud on the pod:
- It’s for a FREE Personal Pan Pizza at Pizza Hut.
- It had a hard expiration: December 31, 1991. Nintendo Player
- Later runs removed the coupon entirely, and even the manual/box callouts disappear. Nintendo Player+1
- This is such a clean “1990 childhood” story beat: you bought the game, and the manual itself is your ticket to dinner.
- 3) In-game advertising: Pizza Hut is literally in the scenery
- This is the part people remember most, because it’s not subtle.
- The NES port includes Pizza Hut signage in the background as part of the city set dressing.
- Some guides even call out moments where Pizza Hut signs fall down during the stage, affecting what happens on-screen. GameFAQs
- Podcast framing ideas:
- It’s “synergy” before we used the word synergy every 11 seconds.
- It also fits the IP so naturally that it almost feels like canon marketing: TMNT is pizza, so Pizza Hut becomes “official turtle fuel.”
- 4) The “afterlife” of the promotion: why it disappears in re-releases
- Fast-forward to modern collections and ports: licensing is the buzzkill.
- Reviews of TMNT: The Cowabunga Collection note the games are preserved as well as they “legally can be,” specifically calling out no Pizza Hut advertisements. GoNintendo
- That lines up with the broader pattern: time-limited brand deals tend to get scrubbed when games are re-released decades later.
- 10. Legacy and where it sits in the TMNT game lineage

 It sets the template for “Konami TMNT beat em up at home,” and directly leads into original console entries that borrow its structure and feel. Wikipedia+1
