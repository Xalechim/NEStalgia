# 458 - Beetlejuice

- "If you've seen one ghost... you haven't seen them all." (tagline from the 1988 film's poster)

## I. Setup & Premise

- This is Beetlejuice for the NES, released in North America on May 10, 1991 (Time Extension)
  - It was developed by Rare and published by LJN. (Wikipedia)
  - It is based on the 1988 Tim Burton film, but it blends in the look and spirit of the animated series, which was on the air at the same time. (Wikipedia)
- The pitch: you are the "ghost with the most," and you are out to scare the living Deetz family out of the Maitlands' house
  - You play the film's sleazier, more villainous version of Beetlejuice, not the cartoon's prankster pal. (Den of Geek)
  - The early levels follow the film. You move through the town and the house hunting for the pieces that let you scare the Deetzes away. Later levels leave the movie behind and go to a sewer, a graveyard and the Waiting Room to the Afterlife.
  - The ending has Beetlejuice bowing to the audience and promoting himself for future jobs. (Den of Geek)
- The deal behind the cartridge
  - Rare was founded in 1985 by Tim and Chris Stamper, who came from the arcade developer behind Ultimate Play the Game. In its first five years it made about 60 games, 47 of them for the NES. (Wikipedia)
  - LJN was the toy and licensing company that wanted a game on every movie and TV license it owned. By 1991 it was owned by Acclaim, which bought it from MCA in 1990 for $30 million. (Wikipedia)

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- It is really two games in one cartridge
  - Side-view platforming levels and top-down overhead levels, and the game flips between them. The side levels are timed jumping with some attacking. The overhead levels play like a light adventure game: find an object, hand it to the right person, open the route out. (TASVideos)
- Scares are your weapons, and you buy them
  - Beetlejuice transforms into things like a skeleton, an ogre or a snake, and each one is a limited-use attack. When the shots run out, it is gone.
  - Getting hit while a scare is active cancels it, so every scare is a resource you have to protect.
- A currency system most NES licensed games did not have
  - You stomp beetles to earn vouchers, then spend them in shops that sit in beetle holes.
  - Beetles come in four colors worth 10, 25, 50 or 75 vouchers, with the red 10-voucher beetle the most common. (Speed Demos Archive)
- Bosses are not beaten by hitting them
  - Scare shots push the bosses off the right side of the screen. Damage alone does nothing. (RetroAchievements guide)
- Story fetch quests with film characters
  - In the overhead house, Barbara needs the Book for the Recently Deceased, Delia needs a mouse and Lydia needs a ring. (RetroAchievements guide)
- It is hard, and I will be upfront about that
  - Speedrunners describe it as very hard to play normally. (TASVideos)
  - A common complaint is that you can fall through what look like solid walls, and that jumping on the wrong enemies hurts you. (GoCollect)

## III. Gameplay Structure

- The core loop
  - Stomp beetles for vouchers, buy scares, clear the level, find the items you need, and get to the exit.
  - In side-view levels you jump, stomp and shoot your way across a stage with a timer. In top-down levels you search rooms and kill off enemies, and the doors open once the room is cleared. If you cannot clear it, the door unlocks on its own after a short wait, which keeps you from getting stuck. (Speed Demos Archive)
- Basic controls, button by button
  - D-pad: move, crouch and climb.
  - A: jump. Holding it with up or down while the menu is open also picks a scare. (Speed Demos Archive)
  - B: stomp. If you have a scare equipped, B fires the scare instead.
  - Start: opens the menu where you pick which scare you are carrying.
  - Select: in the top-down house levels, it picks up and drops items and hands them to a character you are facing. (RetroAchievements guide)
- The level run, as speedrunners break it down (TASVideos)
  - Town, Sewers, Basement, House, Attic, Graveyard, Back in the Attic, and the Waiting Room to the Afterlife.
- The finish
  - There is no final boss. In the Waiting Room you collect numbers 1 through 6, which is a nod to the film's afterlife bureaucracy, and then you walk out the exit.
- Yellow flowers give you roughly 15 seconds of invincibility. Pits are instant death.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- The scare list, with attack counts (Speed Demos Archive, TASVideos)
  - Medusa head: 1 attack, 100 vouchers.
  - Birdman: 2 attacks and extra jump height, 150.
  - Two-headed man: 3 attacks, 200. It one-hits the sandworm.
  - Skeleton: 5 attacks and no bee damage, 250. You need it to get past the beehives in the first level.
  - Umbrella head: 9 attacks, 400.
  - Ogre: 12 attacks, 500. It one-hits the cave monsters in the sewer.
  - Ghost: 15 attacks, 600.
  - Snakeman: 19 attacks, 750.
- The sandworm detour
  - In the house, a floating door sends you to Saturn for a sandworm fight. It is the game reaching for the film's most famous monster. The sandworm also guards an exit door that you have to clear.
- A town section with a bug sprayer boss, a sewer ride that ends with a football player kicking footballs at you, and a graveyard boss, a wizard who throws lightning. (TASVideos, RetroAchievements guide)
- The shop stock quirk
  - Which items a shop offers depends on where Beetlejuice enters it. (Speed Demos Archive)
- Hammer, fire extinguisher and spray can
  - In the top-down house you smash moving sculptures with a hammer, put out a fireplace with an extinguisher, and clear a guard with a spray can. (RetroAchievements guide)
- The warp glitch
  - Speedrunners found that pressing all four directions at once shoves Beetlejuice one screen to the right in the overhead levels. A normal controller cannot do it, so it only exists in tool-assisted runs. That helped bring a run down to 7:23. (TASVideos)

## V. History and Development

- Rare and the NES
  - After tech demos impressed Nintendo of America's president, Minoru Arakawa, Rare was given an unlimited budget to make NES games. (Wikipedia)
  - Dave Thomas has said that he spent about 18 months on NES demos before the company released anything. (GamesTM interview)
- Rare and LJN
  - Rare's LJN games include Who Framed Roger Rabbit (1989), A Nightmare on Elm Street (October 1990), WWF WrestleMania Challenge (November 1990) and Beetlejuice (May 1991). (Time Extension, Wikipedia)
- LJN's story
  - Jack Friedman founded LJN in 1967. It grew by licensing toys from TV and movies, including E.T., Jaws and Back to the Future. In 1985 MCA bought a majority share. (Wikipedia)
  - LJN entered video games in 1987 with NES titles made by Atlus, Beam Software and Rare. By 1990, at least 75 percent of its roughly $70 million in sales came from NES and Game Boy games. Acclaim closed it in 1994. (Wikipedia)
- The timing
  - The game arrived three years after the movie, right as the cartoon was ending its ABC run. (GoCollect)
  - Battletoads arrived from Rare in 1991 too. In 1994 Nintendo bought a quarter of the company, and in 2002 Microsoft bought it for $375 million. (Wikipedia)

## VI. Music and Presentation

- Graphics
  - The opening screen is the best-looking part, with a fairly good rendering of Michael Keaton's ghost. (GoCollect)
  - The character sprites look more like the cartoon than the film. (Beetlejuice Wiki)
  - The weak spot is clarity. The screen only scrolls vertically when you are standing on something, and a floor that is not on screen does not exist to the game, so dropping off the bottom is instant death. (TASVideos, RetroAchievements guide)
  - Critics have knocked the graphics and said it barely resembles the movie. (Beetlejuice Wiki)
- Music
  - The music is credited to David Wise, who was Rare's only in-house musician from 1985 to 1994. (Beetlejuice Wiki, Wise credit lists)
  - It does not use Danny Elfman's film theme.
  - Opinions split. Some praise it despite not fitting the film's tone. One review calls it annoying and monotonous. (GoCollect)

## VII. Pop Culture

- The film, 1988
  - Released March 30, 1988, with a $15 million budget, about $1 million of it for effects. It opened to $8,030,897, an Easter weekend record at the time. (Wikipedia)
  - Burton's first choice for Betelgeuse was Sammy Davis Jr. Michael Keaton was suggested by the studio and Burton agreed quickly. It won the 1989 Academy Award for Best Makeup. (Wikipedia)
  - The Day-O dinner party scene is the film's signature moment. Harry Belafonte's calypso song comes out of possessed dinner guests, and the original song in the scene was an Ink Spots tune. (Gold Radio)
  - VHS came out October 19, 1988, and it was the fifth most-rented film in the US in 1989. (Wikipedia)
- The cartoon
  - It ran on ABC from September 9, 1989 to October 26, 1991, with 94 episodes. It won the 1990 Daytime Emmy for Outstanding Animated Program, tied with The New Adventures of Winnie the Pooh. (Wikipedia)
  - Beetlejuice became Lydia's best friend and a prankster, not the villain.
- Merchandise
  - Kenner made 6-inch Beetlejuice figures in 1989 and 1990. Sales were weak, so Kenner followed with a Neighbourhood Nasties set and a Gross Out Meter playset in 1990. (Toy Tales)
  - Dart made a 100-card trading card set in 1990, with 20 glow-in-the-dark stickers. Burger King Kids' Meals had cartoon figures. (collector listings, Wikipedia)
- Michael Keaton and Tim Burton, 1989 to 1991
  - Batman opened June 23, 1989. Burton cast Keaton as Batman partly because he had worked with him on Beetlejuice, and the success of Beetlejuice helped get Batman made. Fans sent tens of thousands of protest letters over Keaton. (Wikipedia)
  - Batman opened to $40.49 million, a record then, and made $411.6 million worldwide. Batmania pushed merchandise past $750 million. (Wikipedia)
- Ghosts and Halloween media around it
  - Ghostbusters II, June 16, 1989, opened to $29.5 million, a record for a non-holiday opening weekend, and the cartoon The Real Ghostbusters influenced it. (Wikipedia)
  - Ghost, July 13, 1990, was the highest-grossing film of 1990 at about $505.7 million, and Whoopi Goldberg won an Oscar for it. (Wikipedia)
  - The Addams Family arrived in late 1991, made about $191.5 million, and had its own video game, a TurboGrafx-16 title. (Wikipedia, Den of Geek)
- Beetlejuice did more than a movie and a game
  - Universal's Beetlejuice's Rock and Roll Graveyard Revue opened in 1992 and ran in Orlando until January 2016. (Orlando Weekly)
  - Beetlejuice the Musical opened on Broadway on April 25, 2019, got eight Tony nominations, and closed January 8, 2023 after 679 performances. (Playbill, New York Theatre Guide)
  - Beetlejuice Beetlejuice arrived September 6, 2024, 36 years later, opening to about $111 million in the US and Canada and making about $452 million worldwide. (Wikipedia)

## VIII. Sequels, Spinoffs and Comparison Games

- Beetlejuice (Game Boy, 1992), also called Horrific Hijinx from the Neitherworld
  - Rare again, with Acclaim publishing under the LJN name. (Wikipedia)
  - It is based on the cartoon, not the film.
  - New: you clear out ghosts you invited into Lydia's home, then rescue Lydia from Astoroth, with a minecart and a Pogosnake. Each of its six levels has a bonus round, mostly puzzle games. (Wikipedia, listings)
- Adventures of Beetlejuice: Skeletons in the Closet (MS-DOS)
  - By Riedel Software Productions and published by Hi Tech Expressions, based on the cartoon.
  - New: you spit loogies at skeletons while Lydia follows behind with a vacuum cleaner. Home of the Underdogs calls it repetitive but says it grows on you.
- Lego Dimensions: Beetlejuice Fun Pack (2017)
  - Added a Beetlejuice minifigure, a Saturn's Sandworm vehicle and a Beetlejuice Adventure World. Beetlejuice's powers include mind control and telekinesis. (LEGO)
- MultiVersus (2024): Beetlejuice joined on August 20, 2024 as an assassin-class fighter. (Gematsu)
- Modern comparison games
  - Shovel Knight (2014): a currency-for-powers shop. Gold buys armor, shovel upgrades and health, and the NES-era design is the reason it works as an homage. (Wikipedia)
  - Blaster Master Zero (2017): a two-view game. It is side-view in the tank and overhead on foot, the same split as this game. (Wikipedia)
  - Poltergeist: A Pixelated Horror (2014): you are a ghost scaring tenants out of your house, with limited powers per level. It is the premise of this game without the license. (Cubed3)
  - Ghost Master (2003): you manage a team of ghosts to haunt mortals, which is the bureaucratic-afterlife side of Beetlejuice. (Wikipedia)
  - Ghostbusters: The Video Game (2009): a licensed movie game that brought back the original cast, which is the version of a licensed game that cares about its source. (Wikipedia)

## IX. Essential Games List

- The case for
  - It is one of the weirdest licensed games on the NES, and the odd design is a feature. A shop, throwaway monsters as weapons and fetch quests all together was not how a movie tie-in was supposed to work.
  - One retrospective called it one of the more interesting NES platformers and said its LJN label overshadows that. (Metal Arcade)
- The case against
  - It is hard in an unfair way. Pits are instant death, and you lose scares to hits.
  - A reviewer said she found it nearly impossible as a seven-year-old and not much easier as an adult. (GoCollect)
  - Angry Video Game Nerd made it a Halloween episode on October 14, 2014. (TVDB)
- One-line verdict suggestion: not essential, but a worthwhile curiosity with more ideas than most LJN games.
