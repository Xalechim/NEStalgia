# NB 054 - Gimmick!


## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: a jealous toy chest kidnaps a little girl, and the newest toy goes after her
  - A father buys his daughter a birthday present. What he actually buys is a live youkai who was wandering the shop and panicked.
  - Yumetaro hid in a bin of pink plush lookalikes and went still. The father picked him precisely because he stood out.
  - The girl adores him. Her five older toys do not. That night they come to life, drag her through a portal into another dimension, and leave Yumetaro behind.
- Yumetaro is not actually a toy, and that is the joke
  - He is a small green oni or youkai with a single horn, enormous eyes, and tiny feet.
  - The horn is not decoration. It is where the stars come from.
- Who made it
  - Developed and published by Sunsoft. Tomomi Sakai directed, designed, and programmed it.
  - Released for Famicom on January 31, 1992.
- The identity wrinkle: this game barely exists in the West
  - No North American release, ever.
  - The only Western release was Scandinavia in May 1993, retitled Mr. Gimmick, imported in tiny quantities by Swedish distributor Bergsala.
  - So a Famicom game most Americans have never touched has a legitimate PAL cartridge with an English manual.
- The PAL manual quietly changes things
  - The girl gets a name in the English manual: Mary. She is unnamed in the Japanese version.
  - The English manual has the father buy Yumetaro from a traveling salesman rather than at a store.
  - The manual also promises you can turn some enemies to your side, which is true and worth flagging early because it is unusual for the genre.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The star is the entire game
  - Hold the button and a star charges above Yumetaro's horn. Release it and it bounces around the screen.
  - It is your only real weapon, and you can only have one out at a time.
  - It is also a vehicle. You can jump onto your own star and ride it to places you cannot otherwise reach.
  - Nearly every hard thing in the game is a star problem, not a jumping problem.
- A real physics engine on a Famicom cartridge
  - Slopes affect the velocity of everything: Yumetaro, enemies, and the star.
  - By the late bosses you are calculating a bounce trajectory while dodging projectiles.
- It was built to embarrass the Super Famicom
  - Sakai's stated goal was a Famicom game that could sit next to 16-bit launch software without apologizing.
  - He got there partly through tile management: the NES's 256 tiles split into two banks of 128 (enemies and protagonists), then subdivided into four groups of 64, which freed up processing for background cogs and floor animation.
  - The result is a game with more incidental animation than almost anything else on the system.
- High Score Board
  - Inside the pirate ship in Stage 2, in the cannon room, there is one enemy that just stands there and never moves.
  - Plug in a second controller and you can drive it around. It still hurts you if it touches you. It cannot kill anything. It cannot leave the room.
  - It does nothing. It rewards nothing. It is not in the manual. Somebody at Sunsoft built a two-player mode for exactly one enemy in exactly one room and then never mentioned it to anyone.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Six stages, six bosses, and a seventh you probably will not see
  - Underground, Pirate Ship, Forest, Ruins, Mines, Tower.
  - Finish those six and you get the bad ending: the toys are beaten, the girl is still gone, and the game restarts from the beginning.
  - The real Stage 7 (Castle) and the real ending are gated behind a much harder condition.
- The good ending condition is brutal
  - Each of the six stages hides a secret area containing one magic item.
  - You need all six. And you need them without using a single continue, since continuing wipes your progress flags back to blank.
  - The stage map shows a colored flag over each stage you cleared with its item intact.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- Momentum and slide
  - You skid a little on every landing. Holding back for a beat after you land kills the slide.
- The bottles
  - Yellow restores health. Pink is a full heal held in reserve for when you are in critical condition.
  - Black gives you a bomb, which arcs and explodes on the ground. Red gives you a fireball, which travels straight.
  - You can only carry three at a time, so you have to spend before you can pick up.
  - Fireballs matter because the star's bounce is unreliable against certain enemies. Bombs matter because at least one obstacle can only be cleared with one.
- Enemies as furniture
  - Most enemies have a safe spot on the head. You can stand on them while charging your next star.
  - Some are outright transportation: a dinosaur in Stage 4 turns docile after a couple of hits and ferries you across the ocean, and there are large birds on the pirate ship that will carry you up to the top sail.
  - There is an ostrich with a rider you can knock off so you can take the ostrich.
- Pure whimsy with no gameplay purpose
  - The spike-shelled tortoises in Stage 2: flip one onto its back and its legs keep churning. Stand on it and it moves you along like a conveyor belt.
  - It accomplishes nothing. Someone just thought it was funny.
  - In Stage 3, little foxes fall out of the trees, hop alongside you, and leave. They can hurt you, but they never attack. They are residents, not enemies.
- Bosses with escape hatches
  - Catch the Stage 2 boss napping and you can shove him off the edge of the ship instead of fighting him.
  - Same trick with a trap-setting creature in Stage 4, who will not even fight back and just dives into a pool.
  - In the European version only, an alarm clock enemy occasionally wanders into the Stage 2 boss fight and rings to wake him up.

## VI. Music & Sound

- The cartridge has a sound chip in it
  - The Japanese cart uses a variant of Sunsoft's FME-7 mapper known as the SUNSOFT 5B, which includes a Yamaha YM2149 PSG.
  - That adds three channels on top of the standard five.
  - Sakai decided to include the expansion specifically because he had already decided who the composer would be and knew the stock Famicom could not reproduce the chords he wanted.
- How Sakai got Kageyama
  - Sakai heard the soundtrack to Out Live on the PC Engine and decided he wanted that composer and nobody else.
  - Sakai was in Nagoya, Kageyama was in Tokyo. They worked over the phone, with Kageyama plugging his Macintosh synthesizer into the handset so Sakai could hear the tracks.
- The soundtrack is not shaped like NES music
  - Pop, acid jazz, fusion, fantasia, hard rock. Kageyama called it a compilation of game music.
  - He described writing it as feeling more like assembling a puzzle than composing.
  - He was pulling from the other staff's tastes: one designer wanted techno and minimalism, one programmer wanted classical.
  - He knew children would be listening to it on loop, so his stated goal was to make sure nothing became grating.
- The Scandinavian version loses it
  - European NES hardware cannot use cartridge sound chips, so the score was rearranged down to the standard five channels.
  - HG101's verdict is generous: it drops from best-sounding game on the system to merely as good as Journey to Silius.
  - The 2002 PlayStation port also sounds different, with cleaner and bassier instruments and largely different drum samples.
- Kageyama died on September 5, 2025, at 62, after a battle with stomach cancer
  - Gimmick! was by far his best known work, and he had been actively involved in its revival, including an official arranged soundtrack.
  - He was also a saxophonist and a photographer.

## VII. Reception, Legacy, and Sequels

- The reviews at the time were bad, and partly wrong
  - Electronic Gaming Monthly, July 1992: 5, 8, 5, 4. Three of the four called it a cutesy kids' game with no variety. The fourth called it a sleeper and said it demands real technique.
  - EGM's own copy said the game has five levels. It has six, or seven counting the secret stage. They were reviewing off a bad fact sheet.
  - Famitsu: 7, 8, 5, 6. Sweden's Nintendomagasinet gave it a 6 and called it an average platformer that would appeal to children.
  - The outlier: Nintendo Magazine System in the UK gave it 91 out of 100 and called it one of the best NES games they had played in a while.
- The distribution problem was the real killer
  - Sakai's Tokyo Toy Show story: buyers would walk up, ask if it was a Super Nintendo game, hear "Famicom," and walk away.
  - He said he had genuinely expected credit for making a next-generation game on last-generation hardware. He got the opposite.
  - His own summary, years later: the game took ten years for people to appreciate it.
- The reappraisal
  - IGN later put it on a list of the ten best NES games of all time, citing the physics.
  - GameSpot's retrospective called the graphics among the prettiest and most detailed of the era and the whole thing a labor of love.
  - HG101 files it alongside Battletoads and Ninja Gaiden in the difficulty tier and tags it a best-game-of-all-time entry.
- It became a collector trophy
  - The Famicom cart is one of the most expensive commercially released games on the system. A 2014 UK valuation guide put it around £320.
  - The PAL cart is PAL-encoded and glitches badly on an NTSC console. An NTSC prototype ROM exists and runs clean, which is how most Americans have actually played it.
- Where it went
  - 2002: PlayStation, in Memorial Series Sunsoft Vol. 6, paired with Battle Formula (Super Spy Hunter).
  - 2020: Gimmick! EXACT MIX, an arcade remake for exA-Arcadia.
  - 2023: Gimmick! Special Edition on Switch, PS4, PC, and Xbox One, with rewind, time attack, and leaderboards. Also on the Evercade Sunsoft collection.
  - 2024: Gimmick! 2, a full sequel.
  - 2025: Limited Run published Mr. Gimmick as an unlicensed NES cartridge, which is the first time it has ever been reasonably purchasable in North America.
- Comparison games
  - Kirby's Adventure: the obvious visual comparison and the exact philosophical opposite. Kirby is a playground. Gimmick! is a test.
  - Little Samson: the other late-generation cute-looking NES platformer that is technically gorgeous, criminally overlooked, and now absurdly expensive.
  - Ufouria: The Saga: the Sunsoft sibling that was rejected for North America for the same reason, and a useful case study in what Sunsoft of America thought Americans wanted.

## VIII. Pop Culture

- The circle closes in the strangest possible way
  - The 2024 sequel was made by Bitwave Games, a Swedish studio founded by a fan of the original.
  - The only reason he could have been a fan is that Bergsala imported a few cartridges into Sweden in 1993.
  - A distribution footnote turned into the only reason the franchise has a modern sequel. Thirty years later, the tiny market that got the game by accident is the one that brought it back.
