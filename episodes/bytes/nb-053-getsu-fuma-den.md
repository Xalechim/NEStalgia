# NB 053 - Getsu Fūma Den

- Hook note: this is a Japan-only 1987 Famicom game with no English box, so there is no real back-of-box tagline to lift. The line above is a stand-in built from the premise; the Japanese campaign actually sold the tech (giant sprites, the lenticular cover), not a slogan. Swap in your own cold open if you find a better one.

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: the last surviving ninja brother walks into hell to take back three stolen swords
  - You play Getsu Fūma, youngest of three brothers from the Getsu clan.
  - The demon lord Ryūkotsuki ("Dragon Master" in the fan translation) rose out of hell, killed the two older brothers, and stole the family's three Pulse Blades (波動剣, Hadōken, the "wave swords").
  - Fūma's job: recover all three blades and kill Ryūkotsuki to avenge his brothers.
- The setting is secretly the far future, not feudal Japan
  - It looks like a samurai-and-demons period piece, but the official premise dates it to the year 14672 A.D., the "first year of the Demon Age."
  - Worth flagging on the show because almost everyone assumes it is set in the past; even some Japanese retro sites joked it had to be a typo for the 1400s.
- Developed and published by Konami, July 7, 1987, Famicom only

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- It is Castlevania-flavored, but set in the Japanese underworld
  - Hero Fūma even vaguely resembles Simon Belmont, and a Famitsu reviewer at the time said it felt like a sequel to Akumajō Dracula (Castlevania).
  - Structurally it sits with Castlevania II, Zelda II, and The Goonies II: a non-linear action-adventure with light RPG growth.
- The lenticular box is the flex
- https://www.reddit.com/r/retrogaming/comments/n43zqe/lenticular_famicom_box_cover_this_is_getsu_fuuma/
  - The front of the box is a lenticular print: tilt it and the artwork changes. Almost no Famicom game went that far on packaging.
  - This is the "you have to see the physical object" detail for the episode.

## III. How It Plays

- Three game modes stitched together
  - A top-down overworld map you walk along set paths (no random encounters), branching to shops, hint shrines, and dungeons.
  - Short side-scrolling action stages behind torii gates: most are under a minute, but there are many, and you replay them every time you cross that spot on the map.
  - Three first-person pseudo-3D dungeons later on, viewed from just behind Fūma's back.
- Controls are simple, the feel is floaty (GameFAQs)
  - Movement is faster and slipperier than Castlevania, with long, mid-air-adjustable jumps. The sword swings in a short downward arc, so reach is a constant problem.
- The light RPG layer: two gauges up top
  - A "sword" meter (your base attack power) climbs the more enemies you kill, basically experience points.
  - A "life" meter is your health.
  - Early on enemies are tanky and you take chip damage constantly; once the sword powers up and you stock items, it eases off fast.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- A toy box of weird weapons and items
  - The war drum fires the kanji for "power" as a short-range projectile.
  - A talisman soaks up some damage; other items grant brief invincibility or clear the screen.
  - There is a spinning-top move that is, frankly, goofy.
  - The three Pulse Blades only become usable once you have all three, at which point they combine into the game's strongest attack.
- Devil Masks as passports
  - To sail between the four islands you need Devil Masks (鬼面符, Kimenfu), earned by beating the island guardians. No mask, no boat.
- The 3D dungeons need graph paper
  - Mapped from behind Fūma, with only a compass for help, they are easy to get lost in.
  - Combat in them is awkward depth-guessing that mostly comes down to mashing. Be honest: this is the part that drags.
- The password screen is pure gibberish (the weird-detail callout)
  - There is no battery save; you write down a 16-character "resurrection spell" made of hiragana that reads like total nonsense.
  - The fun bit: Japanese players still quote their final-boss passwords from memory decades later, strings of syllables that mean nothing yet got burned into their brains forever. That is your High Score Board moment for this game.

## V. Music & Presentation

- Composed by Hidenori Maezawa, hidden behind a joke credit
  - Maezawa also scored Castlevania, Salamander/Life Force, and Gradius II, and he is credited here under a pun pseudonym rather than his name.
  - The soundtrack is a Konami highlight; the catch is there are only a handful of tracks, so they wear thin over a long playthrough.
- Does this look ugly on purpose
  - Lots of harsh greens and reds and clashing backgrounds, a deliberately grimy, uncomfortable hell. It looks like nothing else on the Famicom, which is the point.

## VI. Legacy, Cameos & Sequels

- Fūma became a Konami mascot without ever getting a real sequel
  - He shows up playable in Konami Wai Wai World and Wai Wai World 2, each with a hell stage built from this game.
  - He and Ryūkotsuki became Yu-Gi-Oh cards (Getsu Fuhma and Ryu Kokki), and the theme got a Pop'n Music arrangement.
  - In 2010 he and a stage from this game appeared as DLC in Castlevania: Harmony of Despair, using the original Famicom art, which is how a lot of Western players first met him.
- The 34-year gap
  - There was no true sequel until GetsuFumaDen: Undying Moon, a roguevania released in 2022 set 1,000 years later. Its early-access bundle even included a port of the 1987 original (menus in English, the rest untranslated).
  - For decades the only way to play in English was a 1998 fan translation.
- Comparison games
  - Castlevania II: Simon's Quest, for the non-linear action-adventure-with-RPG-bits shape.
  - The Goonies II, which also mixed side-scrolling with simple first-person mazes (Getsu Fūma Den's are more ambitious, and add combat).
  - The identity problem: is it a Genpei Tōma Den clone?
    - Namco's 1986 arcade game Genpei Tōma Den is strikingly similar: same era, same Japanese-hell vibe, a running swordsman fighting giant demons, even a similar death scene.
    - Konami's own staff later admitted the influence (HG101), so it is fair to call it Konami's answer to Genpei rather than a rip-off, with far more exploration and RPG layering bolted on.
