# NB 051 - Puyo Puyo


## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: Pairs of colored blobs fall from the top of the screen. Connect four or more of the same color and they pop. Chain multiple pops together and you bury your opponent in puyo blobby garbage. The game that made falling-block puzzlers competitive.
- The origin story has layers.
- Compile — a studio known primarily for excellent shoot-'em-ups like Zanac, Aleste, and M.U.S.H.A. — first released Puyo Puyo in 1991 on the MSX2 and Famicom Disk System. The Disk System version was bundled with Tokuma Shoten's Famimaga magazine as a pack-in, and it was the second-to-last game ever released for the FDS.
- In 1992, Compile and Sega retooled it for arcades on Sega's System C-2 hardware, transforming it into a head-to-head versus game modeled on the fighting game boom.
- The Famicom cartridge version followed on July 23, 1993, incorporating the competitive two-player mode that had made the arcade version a hit. That's the version we're covering
- The designer, Kazunari Yonemitsu felt that puzzle games of the era had forgettable characters and no personality. His solution was to pull characters from Compile's existing Madou Monogatari dungeon-crawler RPG series — a first-person dungeon crawl where the puyos were just regular slime enemies (Think Dragon Quest).
- He also cited Street Fighter II as a direct influence, trying to capture that game's competitive intensity in a puzzle framework.
- The result was a puzzle game that borrowed its roster from an RPG and its structure from a fighting game, built by a studio that made shooters. Three genres colliding to produce a fourth

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The chain system holds up
- The core mechanic: puyos fall in pairs. Match four or more of the same color and they vanish. When they vanish, everything above them drops — and if the newly settled puyos form another group of four, those pop too. That's a chain. Chains are the currency of aggression — the longer your chain, the more garbage puyos (colorless, obstructive dead weight) you dump onto your opponent's field. A two-chain is a nudge. A five-chain is a serious threat.
- The depth is in how chains are constructed. You don't stumble into a five-chain. You build one — deliberately placing colors in a specific stacking order so that each collapse triggers the next, like a row of dominoes made of jelly. You're essentially playing the game in reverse: imagining the cascade before it happens, placing puyos now for a payoff six or seven moves down the line. It's closer to chess than it is to Tetris. Where Tetris rewards spatial efficiency and reaction speed, Puyo Puyo rewards foresight and architectural planning
- The skill ceiling is effectively unlimited. Top-level competitive players routinely construct 11-, 12-, 13-chains as standard play. No other puzzle game on any platform from this era offers a comparable depth of skill expression, and few from any era do
- The character presentation holds up
- Before each single-player fight, protagonist Arle Nadja has a brief, comedic exchange with her upcoming opponent.
- Skeleton T is a skeleton obsessed with tea.
- Nasu Grave is an eggplant in armor.
- Draco Centauros is a vain dragon girl fixated on beauty pageants.
- Satan — literally named Satan — is the final boss who has an unrequited crush on Arle
- Compile understood that competition needs an antagonist, not just a score counter
- The Famicom port specifically mixed
- The 1993 Famicom cartridge added a "LIMIT" option not present in the arcade version, letting players set the maximum garbage output per chain to various thresholds — from modest caps all the way up to 255 (the maximum value an 8-bit register can hold). This tuning dial lets you control how snowball-heavy matches get, which is a meaningful addition for a home version meant to be played in a living room rather than an arcade
- The honest trade-off: by mid-1993, Puyo Puyo already existed on the Mega Drive, Game Gear, and PC-98, and a Super Famicom version was just a few months away. The Famicom port can't match those versions visually, but the chain system, the garbage mechanics, the versus structure — all of it is intact.
- The Madou Monogatari parentage context
- Madou Monogatari was a first-person dungeon-crawler RPG series that Compile launched on the MSX in 1989. The puyos were just rank-and-file enemies. Arle was the adventurer. Carbuncle — the little yellow creature that became the franchise mascot — was her companion. When Puyo Puyo exploded in popularity, the spin-off swallowed its parent whole. By the mid-'90s, new Madou Monogatari releases were marketing themselves as "Puyo Puyo RPGs" just to get noticed. The child outgrew the parent so completely that most players have never heard of the series that spawned it.
- Not exactly similar, but kinda like how Persona is more popular than Shin Megami Tensei

## III. The Franchise — Every Major Rule Change

- Puyo Puyo's story from here is a story about iterative design. Each major entry introduced at least one systemic change that reshaped how the game played from casual to tournament level:
- Puyo Puyo Tsu (1994) — The sequel, and for most competitive players, the definitive version. Its signature addition: the offset rule. In the original, once garbage was headed your way, it was coming — you had no recourse. Tsu introduced the ability to fire a counter-chain while garbage was pending. Your chain would cancel out some or all of the incoming garbage, and if your chain exceeded what was sent, the surplus would boomerang back to the sender.
- Puyo Puyo SUN (1996) — The title puns on the Japanese word for "three" (san/sun). SUN introduced Sun Puyos — special pieces that appear on your field when you successfully offset incoming garbage. Clearing Sun Puyos alongside regular chains adds bonus damage to your attack.
- Puyo Puyo~n (1999) — The fourth mainline game. Its innovation was character-specific special abilities, borrowed directly from that Street Fighter 2 inspo. Each character had a unique power that could alter the board state — move puyos, transform colors, etc. This was Compile's most experimental swing, and the most polarizing.
- Puyo Pop Fever (2003) — The first entry developed by Sonic Team after Sega acquired the Puyo Puyo rights in 1998 amid Compile's mounting debt. Compile went bankrupt later that same year, with staff scattering to successor studios Compile Heart, MileStone, and others.
- Fever's signature addition was its namesake mode: a comeback mechanic triggered by successfully offsetting garbage. When activated, Fever cleared your board and dropped a series of pre-built, nearly-complete chains that you could trigger by finding the right "key" piece.
- So Fall behind, trigger Fever, mount a massive counter-attack.
- It also replaced nearly the entire character roster with a new cast set in a new world called Primp — a soft reboot that divided longtime fans. An odd piece of hardware history: the Dreamcast version was Sega's final first-party game for that console
- Puyo Puyo Tetris (2014) — The crossover that brought Puyo Puyo to mainstream audiences for real. Players could choose to play either Puyo Puyo (using Tsu rules) or Tetris, compete against each other across different games, or play hybrid modes where Puyo and Tetris pieces fell on the same board or players swapped between separate boards on a timer.
- The Western disguises — RESKINS
- Dr. Robotnik's Mean Bean Machine (1993).
- Nintendo did the same thing two years later, swapping in Kirby characters for the Super Famicom version and releasing it as Kirby's Avalanche (1995)

## IV. Pop Culture, Legacy & Connections

- Compile's fate: A shmup studio created one of the most important puzzle franchises ever made, got consumed by its success, sold the IP to stay alive, and went bankrupt anyway. Sega acquired the Puyo Puyo rights in 1998, but Compile retained a license to keep making games through 2002. When the deadline passed with no replacement IP capable of sustaining the company, Compile filed for bankruptcy in November 2003. The franchise that defined them outlived them by decades and is still producing new entries today under Sonic Team's stewardship. Over 10 million copies sold across the series' lifetime, not counting the Compile-era games that predate Sega's tracking
- The Tetris comparison: Both are falling-block puzzle games. Both became cultural touchstones. Both have competitive scenes. But the design philosophies are fundamentally opposed. Tetris is about efficiency — fill lines, clear space, survive the increasing speed. Puyo Puyo is about investment — build structures now for explosive payoffs later. Tetris punishes you for leaving gaps. Puyo Puyo rewards you for leaving gaps in exactly the right places. Tetris is accounting. Puyo Puyo is demolition architecture. The fact that Puyo Puyo Tetris works as a competitive crossover is proof that the two systems are complementary rather than redundant — different enough to create genuine asymmetry, similar enough to share a screen
- Thematic relatives:
- Super Puzzle Fighter II Turbo (1996, Capcom) — the most direct spiritual descendant. Capcom took the competitive versus structure Puyo pioneered, added crash gems and Street Fighter/Darkstalkers characters, and made the other great versus puzzler of the '90s.
