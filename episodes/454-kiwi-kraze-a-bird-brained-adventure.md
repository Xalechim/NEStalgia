# 454 - Kiwi Kraze: A Bird-Brained Adventure

- Wally's got himself a kiwi cookbook, and your friends are the main ingredient!

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: a flightless bird steals a balloon and commits twenty counts of grand theft aviation
  - You are Tiki, a kiwi bird. A large blue sea mammal named Wally has raided the zoo, made off with all of Tiki's friends, and is selling them to other zoos across New Zealand.
  - Tiki was the only one who got away. So he goes and gets them back, one cage at a time, across twenty stages.
  - His girlfriend, Phee-Phee, is the last one. She is at the end of the game, behind the final boss.
- The identity problem: this game has two names and neither one is quite right
  - Everywhere else in the world, the NES cartridge is called The NewZealand Story, published by Ocean.
  - In North America, Taito published it as Kiwi Kraze: A Bird-Brained Adventure!
  - So an American kid and a British kid could own the identical game and have no idea they were talking about the same thing. Not too uncommon actually STAR FOX vs LYLAT WARS
- It is an arcade port, and a good one, from an unexpected place
  - Arcade original: Taito, 1988.
  - NES port: developed by Software Creations, a British studio, released March 1991.
  - A Japanese arcade game about New Zealand, converted by a British team, sold in America under a name that mentions neither Japan nor New Zealand.
- The simplest way to frame it for listeners
  - This is not a run-to-the-right platformer. Do not picture Mario.
  - Picture Bubble Bobble's cuteness stretched across huge scrolling mazes, where the exit could be anywhere and you have to go find it.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The mazes go in every direction, and that is the whole point
  - Stages are not corridors. They scroll all four ways and sprawl outward.
  - The goal in every level is the same: find the caged kiwi. The map does not tell you how to get there, only where "there" is.
  - The levels are built out of tiny 8x8 tiles rather than the 16x16 tiles most platformers of the era used, so you get dense, fiddly, puzzle-like little structures connected by wide open flying spaces.
- You get around by mugging your enemies for their vehicles
  - Most enemies fly around on balloons. Shoot the enemy, jump on the balloon, it is yours now.
  - You can also jump onto a balloon while the enemy is still standing on it, which knocks him off
- Most enemies cannot hurt you by touching you
  - Enemies are mostly safe to bump into. It is their weapons that kill you.
  - That flips the normal platformer instinct. You are not avoiding bodies, you are avoiding projectiles.
- It is much harder than it looks
  - Tiki dies in one hit. Spikes, drowning, any projectile.
  - The cute pastel art is doing a lot of work to disguise a fairly brutal game.
- High Score Board: nobody can agree what the villain is
  - The final boss has a species crisis that spans the entire history of this game.
  - Taito's arcade closing credits identify him as a leopard seal. The Japanese material calls him Azarashi, which is just the Japanese word for seal.
  - Ocean's home computer packaging called him a walrus.
  - And the American NES manual goes all in: he is "Wally Walrus" throughout, complete with a warning that Wally and his nasty pals hate jumping.
  - So depending on which box you bought and which continent you were on, the villain of this game is either a leopard seal or a walrus, and Taito apparently never got a memo about it.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Five worlds, four levels each, twenty stages, one caged friend per level
  - Reach the cage, free the kiwi, next stage. That is the loop.
  - Most worlds end in a boss. World Four ends in a spike-filled ship interior instead of an actual boss fight (GameFAQs).
- Basic controls, on land
  - D-pad left and right: move. Up and down do nothing on land (GameFAQs).
  - A: jump. Hold longer for a higher jump. Tap A while falling to flap Tiki's wings and slow the descent (Take on the NES Library).
  - B: fire your weapon. Default is a bow and arrow with unlimited ammo.
  - Start: pause, which also brings up the map overlay showing level size, your position, and the exit (NES manual).
  - Select on the title screen: one or two players. It is alternating play, so it is not much of a co-op.
- Basic controls, on a balloon
  - Hold A to rise. Let go to sink. The balloon falls under Tiki's weight, so you are constantly feathering it.
  - D-pad still moves you horizontally, and you can still shoot while flying.
  - Press Down while resting on the ground to dismount (Take on the NES Library).
- Basic controls, underwater
  - D-pad moves you in all eight directions. A and B do nothing while submerged.
  - Your score display switches to an air meter that drains.
  - Surface to breathe. On the surface, B spits water at an upward angle, which both attacks enemies and refills your air faster (Take on the NES Library).
  - Long water sections require you to plan around air pockets.
- The weapon and item loop
  - Three weapons: bow and arrow (default), bombs that fall on an angle, and the green laser gun.
  - The laser is the one to hunt for. It fires straight, fires fast, and shoots through walls, which lets you clear a room you have not entered yet (GameFAQs).
  - You keep a weapon until you die or finish the stage.
  - Magic items on top of that: a book that wipes the screen, an invincibility pickup, and the joystick, which is the interesting one.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- You can jump up through floors, and this rewires the level design
  - Tiki passes upward through solid floors. Take on the NES Library notes this is a Taito habit, since Bubble Bobble works the same way.
  - You can even jump up into walls from underneath. You only land if Tiki is not stuck partway inside the geometry.
  - Consequence: climbing up is easy, getting back down is hard, and areas that look sealed off are often reachable if you jump at the right spot.
  - This is the mechanic that makes the mazes work. It is also the one that makes players think they are stuck when they are not.
- The joystick item, which is a quiet game-changer
  - Normally flying means constantly tapping and holding A to fight gravity.
  - The joystick powerup gives you direct D-pad control of the balloon in all directions, no A button required.
  - There are shortcuts in the game that only open up with the precision this gives you (Take on the NES Library). Losing the balloon loses the powerup.
- Balloons are not interchangeable
  - Several types with different behavior. The common sheep-head one is your workhorse.
  - There is a tall red one that accelerates upward very slowly but is the only balloon immune to spikes.
  - A green ostrich-looking one accelerates fastest. A carriage type is what you respawn in after dying while flying (Take on the NES Library).
- The invisible timer and the Red Devil
  - There is no visible clock. Take too long and you get a "Hurry Up" warning.
  - Then an invincible red devil spawns and chases you. He passes through walls. You cannot kill him.
  - He does move at a steady speed, so you can outrun him if you have room, which is more mercy than most hurry-up enemies offer.
  - The GameFAQs writer flags this constantly, once per world, because it genuinely shapes how you play.
- Invisible warps you have to shoot into existence
  - Some levels hide warp windows in empty air. Shoot at the spot repeatedly and a black window appears.
  - The tell: if your arrows are stopping in mid-air, keep firing (Take on the NES Library).
  - Some lead to bonus rooms with apples and extra lives. Some skip you ahead a level or more. The 1-4 warp lets you skip a boss entirely (GameFAQs).
  - Nothing in the game tells you these exist. This is pure arcade-era "somebody at school told me" design.
- The one that will make people quit: the World Four rule change
  - For the first three worlds, dying puts you back where you died with a moment of invincibility.
  - Starting in World Four, that goes away. Death sends you to the beginning of the stage.
  - Take on the NES Library beat World Three within a few tries and described everything after that as wading through mud. That is the difficulty curve in one sentence.
  - You get three lives and three continues. Then you are back at the title screen.
- Honest gripes
  - Hitboxes get strange on a balloon. The balloon becomes the contact point, so you can graze spikes with Tiki's head and be fine, then die instantly to the same contact on foot.
  - Some of the tiny spikes do not read clearly against the detailed backgrounds.
  - The last stage is on ice, so you have no traction and stop only by hitting a wall, in a level that is already asking for pixel-precise jumps between spikes (GameFAQs).

## V. History & Development

- It started as a sequel to a completely different game
  - Gaming Hell reports the project began as a follow-up to Taito's much older arcade game Crazy Balloon, under the working title Crazy Balloon Part II.
  - They cite the Taito Memories Pocket manual and a Hamster developer stream as confirmation.
  - Once you know that, the balloon-stealing mechanic stops looking like a quirk and starts looking like the leftover skeleton of the original concept.
- The setting came from somebody's vacation
  - The commonly repeated origin, traced back to a Computer and Video Games piece, is that a Taito programmer went on holiday to New Zealand and wanted to make a game about it.
  - Outside the map between stages and place names like Auckland, Rotorua, and Mount Cook, the environments have very little to do with the real country.
  - Also, Tiki looks nothing like a real kiwi. He is basically an anime chick in sneakers. HG101 points out the game knows this, because some background posters show a realistic kiwi silhouette right next to him.
- Fresh off Bubble Bobble
  - Taito was riding the worldwide success of Bubble Bobble and clearly wanted another cute platformer with a rescue-the-girlfriend plot (Hardcore Gaming 101).
  - The DNA shows: EXTEND letters, jumping through floors, an escalating hurry-up enemy.
- The NES port is better than it had any right to be
  - What got cut: some enemies, weapons, and vehicles, and the boss sprites are shrunk down.
  - What survived: almost all the warps and secret areas, plus the New Zealand map between rounds, which only the NES and Master System ports kept.
  - What did not survive: the Heaven stages.
- About those Heaven stages, since they are worth describing on air even though the NES cut them
  - In the arcade, if you lost your last life in the middle chunk of the game to a solid projectile, Tiki went to Heaven, and Heaven was a playable level made of clouds where every enemy had a halo.
  - Reach the exit and you meet a goddess figure and the game ends, but the message hints there is a way out.
  - Find the hidden escape route and you get put back into the game one stage past where you died. One time only (Hardcore Gaming 101).
  - NES players never got any of this. It is the single biggest thing the port lost.
- Port trivia worth one line
  - The Japanese Mega Drive version was built from the prototype arcade board, so its levels are different from every other release, and it has only thirteen stages instead of twenty.

## VI. Music & Presentation

- The Follin brothers did the NES soundtrack
  - Tim and Geoff Follin, both at Software Creations, handled the NES music.
  - Hardcore Gaming 101 goes further than usual and says their version outclasses the soundtrack of every other version of this game, arcade included.
  - Callback for the show: the Follins also did the NES Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade, which we have already covered.
- Important nuance: they were arranging, not composing from scratch
  - The underlying compositions came from the Taito arcade team (credited variously to Hisayoshi Ogura, Yasuko Yamada, and Yasuhisa Watanabe depending on the source).
  - The Follin tracks are documented as arrangements of the arcade themes (WhoSampled). That does not diminish the work, since the whole Follin reputation is built on wringing sounds out of hardware nobody thought could produce them.
- The catch nobody mentions until they have played it
  - There is one in-game song. For all twenty levels.

## VII. Pop Culture

- The kiwi is a national symbol carrying a lot of weight
  - Te Ara, New Zealand's official encyclopedia, describes the kiwi as an unlikely national emblem: flightless, nocturnal, rarely seen in the wild by the people who put it on their coins and stamps.
  - New Zealanders are called Kiwis. The bird is a taonga, a treasure, in Maori culture.
  - So this is not a random cute animal mascot. Taito picked the single most loaded animal in the country.
- The timing is genuinely poignant, and this is the best hook in the episode
  - The NES version came out in 1991. In that same year, the Bank of New Zealand, the Department of Conservation, and the Royal Forest and Bird Society launched the Kiwi Recovery Programme (Department of Conservation).
  - The first formal kiwi recovery plan covered 1991 to 1996.
  - So in the exact year an American kid was buying a cartridge about a kiwi rescuing other kiwis from cages, New Zealand was standing up a national program because real kiwi populations were collapsing.
  - The cause was different, of course. Real kiwi are killed by introduced stoats, dogs, and ferrets, not by a cartoon walrus running a black market. But the shape of the story is the same: the national bird is in trouble and somebody has to go get it back.
  - Kiwi numbers are still declining today, roughly two percent a year in unmanaged areas.

## VIII. Sequels, Spinoffs, and Comparison Games

- There is no real sequel, only remakes
  - Tiki never got a proper follow-up.
  - Cameos: Liquid Kids, Bubble Symphony, Pop'n Pop, and Space Invaders DX.
  - New Zealand Story Revolution, Nintendo DS, 2007. Reviews were middling. IGN gave it 5.9, Eurogamer 6 out of 10, both criticizing the touchscreen additions and some spiteful level design.
  - The NewZealand Story: Untold Adventure, a Steam remake, released February 2026. Early reception was mixed to negative, with complaints about collision detection and stages missing elements.
  - The arcade original is easy to get now: Taito Legends compilations, and Hamster's Arcade Archives release for Switch and PS4 in January 2023.

## IX. Essential Games List Vote

- Case for Essential
  - Strongest argument: this is the rare NES arcade port that is arguably better than its arcade parent in at least one dimension, and it hides a real design idea (a cute platformer built as an open maze) that almost nothing else on the console attempted.
  - Supporting: the Follin soundtrack alone is a piece of NES history.
  - Supporting: it is a Taito game with clear Bubble Bobble lineage that most American players have never heard of, which is exactly the kind of gap this list should be filling.
- Case against Essential
  - Strongest argument: the difficulty design is unfair in a way that is not interesting, specifically the World Four rule change that strips checkpointing right when the levels get most punishing.
  - Supporting: one song for twenty levels is a real endurance problem on a game this long.
  - Supporting: the secrets are invisible and undiscoverable without a guide, which was fine in an arcade and is less fine on a cartridge you own.
  - Supporting: "essential" should probably mean an American NES player in 1991 could reasonably have encountered it, and almost nobody did.
- Host note
  - The honest verdict may be that this is essential to understanding what the NES could do and not essential to the average player's experience of the console. Worth saying out loud either way.
