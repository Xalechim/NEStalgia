# 465 - Castelian

- "Julius is his name. Demolition is his game."

&nbsp;

## I. Setup & Premise

- What it is based on: Nebulus, a 1987 home computer game designed by John M. Phillips for Hewson Consultants
  - Nebulus came out in 1987 on the ZX Spectrum, Commodore 64 and other British home computers, and the Amiga and Atari ST followed. In America it was sold as Tower Toppler. Castelian is the name the NES and Game Boy ports got.
  - So this is a console port of a computer classic, just like Times of Lore. The NES version is a rework, not a copy, with a new hero, new story dressing and a new bonus stage.
- Who made it
  - Bits Studios developed the NES and Game Boy versions, and David Whittaker did the music.
  - Triffix Entertainment published it in North America. Hiro published the Famicom version in Japan on December 11, 1992, under the title Kyoro-chan Land. Storm published the PAL version in Europe.
- The story, as the NES manual tells it
  - The manual opens on a sunny morning in Jemmerville, an island nation on the planet Centrus, where diamonds and sapphires are mined and the whole Triangulum galaxy envies the gems.
  - Julius is asleep in his terraineum (terrarium?) when a hologram of the president of Domoloco and Sons Inc. wakes him. The governor says alien towers are surrounding the island, spaceports are almost cut off, and one more tower will seal it in completely. Nobody knows what the towers are or where they came from. The boss wants them demolished.
  - Julius is an "Anurian Hybrid." Anura is the scientific order that includes frogs, so that is a frog joke. He wants to get back to his Sunday snooze.
- The pitch
  - Climb eight cylindrical towers, plant a bomb at the top of each one and collapse it, then run a bonus stage between towers. The tower turns as you walk around it.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The tower turns as you walk
  - You do not scroll left to right. Walk left or right and the whole cylinder rotates, so ledges, doorways and enemies swing in from the edges. The look of a round 3D object on 8-bit hardware is the reason anyone remembers this game.
- Doors go through the tower
  - Press Up at a doorway and Julius walks through the tower to the opposite side. Press Up again to come back out. It is how you skip the monsters stuck on the near side.
- The elevators sink
  - You step on a lit block, press Up and ride it, but you have to hop off quickly. The elevator drops back down after a few seconds, even if you do not press Down. (NES manual)
- The tower fights you
  - Some ledges crumble as you walk on them. The manual blames the strong gravity of Centrus for pulling you down a stairway if you stop to rest. Falling into the sea is fatal, and the manual says Anurian Hybrids cannot swim.
- Time is the real enemy
  - There is a countdown on screen, and the manual says if Julius runs out of time, so might Jemmerville. Time left over at the end of a tower converts to bonus points, so it is a speed game too.
- A bonus stage that gives you time
  - In the diamond-studded bonus level you hop across clouds and pits collecting gems. The manual says you can win lost time back, and that you do not have to collect every gem to reach the clock tower at the end.
- Two difficulty levels
  - Novice is the default and Hero is harder. On Hero you earn extra lives at 10,000 points instead of 5,000, the timer ticks faster and the chasing enemy moves twice as fast.
- High Score Board
  - The manual's story says seven alien towers are surrounding the island. A few pages later, the section called Taking on the Towers says there are eight towers to be demolished. So the game is either a tower short or the boss cannot count. The NES version has eight towers.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- The core loop
  - Start at the bottom of a tower. Climb stairs and elevators ledge by ledge, avoid or shoot the things that patrol the tower, use doorways to shortcut around hazards, reach the top and blow up the tower. Then play the bonus level and move on to the next tower.
  - Julius starts with three lives. An extra life comes every 5,000 points on Novice. Contact with an enemy knocks him down off his ledge. If there is no ledge below, he lands in the sea. (NES manual)
  - Getting hit costs you height, not just a life, and a reviewer says one misstep can send you several stages back.
  - The screen shows lives, time remaining, score and the tower number.
- Controls (NES manual, Questicle)
  - D-pad Left and Right: walk around the tower.
  - D-pad Up: enter a doorway, or go up on an elevator.
  - D-pad Down: go down on an elevator. On the Japanese version, Down at a doorway takes the alternate exit. In the American version it is B plus Up.
  - A: shoot while standing still, or jump while walking. That is why a reviewer says jumping needs a direction held down together with A.
  - B: not used in the manual's diagram. Questicle says it does nothing.
  - Select: opens the options on the title screen, where you choose one or two players, music or sound effects, and Novice or Hero.
  - Start: starts the game and pauses it.
- Enemies
  - The manual names Hexalons, I-Balls, Tri-Zeroop and Metallii, and says all of them are invincible. Some things can be stunned, and orbiting Spheroids can be blown apart. Carbonobombs are described as made of a top-secret compound harder than diamond.
  - Your real defense is to run away or slip through a doorway. (NES manual)

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- Only four enemies at a time
  - The game keeps at most four enemies active on a tower. New ones spawn as you scroll and older ones despawn when they fall off the bottom. A reviewer found you can steer the spawns by when you kill things, and joked that the player ends up programming the game. (Speed Demos Archive, rec0ded88)
- The timed chaser
  - After a countdown, if fewer than four enemies are active, a molecule enemy spawns. Hero doubles its speed. It is why standing still to plan a jump is dangerous.
- Tower 7 has no doors
  - Towers 1 to 6 and tower 8 each have a door with two exits. The second exit is usually a shortcut but not always. Tower 7 has none, so you have to climb the whole thing. (Speed Demos Archive)
- The tile at the top
  - Each tower has a top tile that, when you press Down on it, spawns an elevator, wipes out every enemy and freezes the timer. It leads to a glitched extension. In tower 7 you can ride it up and down to farm score. 

## V. History and Development

- The original
  - John M. Phillips designed and programmed Nebulus. He first prototyped a rotating horizontal cylinder on the ZX Spectrum, then switched to the vertical cylinder on the Commodore 64 that we know. Andrew Hewson remembered it as a spinning cylinder with sprites running over it. 
- Kyoro-chan Land
  - In Japan the lead became Kyorochan, the parrot mascot of Morinaga's ChocoBall candy. The jewels became Chocoballs, enemy graphics changed, and the Famicom version added a password system and a pause feature. (Wikipedia)

## VII. Pop Culture

- Home computers in the late 1980s
  - Hewson was one of the great British publishers of the 8-bit era, with Uridium, Paradroid and Cybernoid on its roster alongside Nebulus. (Wikipedia, C64-Wiki)
  - Nebulus won the Zzap!64 Gold Medal, a Smash from Crash, a Megagame from Your Sinclair and a Mastergame from Amstrad Action. Wikipedia credits it with Best Original Game at the Golden Joystick Awards. Resident Evil Requiem won the 2026 Golden Joystick. Your Sinclair ranked the Spectrum version number 30 on its Top 100, and Amiga Power put the Amiga version at number 14 of all time. (Wikipedia)
- Mid 1991 on the other consoles
  - The SNES gave the period its own rotating-world trick with Mode 7. Castelian's tower is the same fantasy done with 8-bit tricks.

## VIII. Sequels, Spinoffs and Comparison Games

- There was no NES sequel. These are the other versions and descendants.
- Nebulus 2: Pogo a Gogo (Amiga, 1991)
  - Infernal Byte developed it, 21st Century Entertainment published it. It added jumping boots, rockets and magnets.
  - Reviews were mixed. Amiga Power's Gary Penn said the sub-games last too long, and Ace said it was not recommended.
- Tower Toppler (Atari 7800, late 1980s, and DOS)
  - The American name for Nebulus. The 7800 version was published by Atari Corp. and is the one with a level select when both difficulty switches are set to Hard.
- Castelian (Game Boy, 1991) and Kyoro-chan Land (Game Boy, Japan 1992)
  - Same Bits Studios port on handheld. Commenters say it is similarly frustrating.
- Re-releases and remakes
  - Commodore 64 Direct-to-TV (2004). Nebulus came to the Wii Virtual Console in Europe on June 13, 2008 and North America on May 4, 2009. There is also a freeware Tower Toppler remake and a fan remake called Nebulous. (Wikipedia, indie retro sites)
- Modern comparison games
  - Fez (Polytron, April 13, 2012, Xbox 360, later Windows and other platforms)
    - A 2D platformer built on rotating a 3D world, so a flat view becomes a different flat view.
    - What it shares: a world that rotates and platforms that line up only from the right angle. The difference: in Fez you rotate the world yourself with the shoulder buttons, in Castelian the tower rotates because you walked.
    - A moment to point at: the first time you rotate a ledge into a path.

## IX. Essential Games List

- Case for Essential
  - It is the rare 8-bit port that keeps the idea that made the original famous. A tower that turns around you is still a great trick, and the bonus stage that buys back time is a good risk and reward.
  - Retro Games Review calls the level design superb, and the game rewards memorizing each tower. The Famicom version has a password, so it is the version to hunt for.
- Case against Essential
  - Questicle gave it an F minus and called it punishing, and the jump and shoot sharing one button gets you the wrong action. Collision is inconsistent, and one slip costs a lot of height.
  - A blog that ranked every version put the NES port near the bottom, 12 out of 30, well below the Archimedes and Atari ST versions. If you want Nebulus, play those or the Wii Virtual Console version.
