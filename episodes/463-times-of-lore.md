# 463 - Times of Lore

- The fantasy role-playing game filled with endless fantastic excitement!

&nbsp;

## I. Setup & Premise

- What it is based on: nothing. It is an original fantasy world called Albareth, made by Chris Roberts at Origin Systems in 1988.
  - Chris Roberts is the same Chris Roberts who made Wing Commander in 1990 (which I played at LI Retro and it was quite fun) and, decades later, Star Citizen. This was his first game for Origin, so the man who gave us space dogfights started out with a knight and a sword.
  - The NES game is a port. The computer original came out in October 1988 on the Amiga, Atari ST and Commodore 64, and later reached DOS, ZX Spectrum, Amstrad CPC and Apple II. (Wikipedia)
- Who made it
  - Origin Systems developed and published the original. Richard Garriott's company, the Ultima people.
  - Toho Co., Ltd. published the NES version. The Famicom version came out in Japan on December 7, 1990, and the American NES release followed in May 1991. (VGMPF)
- The story
  - Twenty years ago High King Valwyn ended a war with the barbarians, got tired, and sailed to the Elden homeland to rest. He promised to come back in twenty years. Those twenty years are up and he has not shown.
  - His steward, Lord Dariel, cannot keep the lords in line. Brigands roam the roads, monsters pour in over the mountains and the barbarians are massing in the south.
  - You are the nephew or niece of an Eralan scholar. Your uncle has just died, so you go to a tavern called the Frothing Slosh to find work. You start the game in an upstairs room in that inn.
- The pitch
  - It is a Zelda-style action game where the plot comes from talking to people. You walk around a big kingdom, ask townsfolk the right questions and run errands that grow into a conspiracy.
  - You pick a knight, a valkyrie or a barbarian. The manual says all three are equal in strength and abilities, so is the choice mostly cosmetic?

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The picture menu
  - Instead of typed commands, you open a row of icons: talk, look, grab, give, hold and your items.
  - On computers in 1988 this was unusual. Richard Garriott's Ultima VI redesign drew on it, cutting roughly 26 commands down to ten icons. The Official Book of Ultima credits the icon interface of Times of Lore as the influence. (Wikipedia)
- Conversation by keyword
  - Talk to someone and a soft bell rings when they mention something important. That word goes into your Question list and you can ask the next person about it.
  - Once you have what you need on a topic, the word drops off the list.
- A real clock
  - Day turns to night, and the screen tints orange at dusk. People sleep at night, and you cannot talk to them until morning.
- Health is a candle
  - The candle in the lower right burns down as you take hits or go hungry. If the flame goes out, you die and go back to your last save.
  - There are no experience points and no levels. Your character does not get stronger, you get better equipment and better information.
- Food and sleep
  - You have to eat. A hunger icon appears after you have walked a while, and you lose life if you have no food. Innkeepers sell food and a bed.
- Stealth and tricks instead of fighting
  - Many of the quests can be solved by slipping past guards, using a ring or a scroll, or finding the right item. Combat is a means of collecting food, potions and scrolls.
- A big world with no loading between towns
  - The computer version advertised about 13,000 screens of map. The NES keeps the kingdom as one continuous world. (Wikipedia, VGMPF)

## III. Gameplay Structure

- The core loop
  - Wake up in the Frothing Slosh, talk to everyone, and a friar sends you off to recover a stolen urn from orcs. That first errand leads to a chain of seven quests across the kingdom.
  - Talk, collect keywords, travel, fight or sneak your way to a location, fetch the item, bring it back. Sleep at an inn to heal and to get a password.
  - The quests, in order, go roughly: recover the Foretelling Stones from an orc camp, fetch the Tablet of Truth from Ganestor castle, find an assassin in Lankwell and get his confession, catch a spy at the gate at night, free a prince from bandits at Shadowpeak, kill a lyche in a ruined desert temple, and finally take down the abbot. (Legacy of Games)
  - There are no town maps. The world is one big overhead map and you walk through town gates and doors. Entering a building takes the roof off so you can see inside.
- Controls
  - D-pad: walk.
  - A: attack. This is Zelda-style, you face an enemy and swing. (Questicle, Legacy of Games)
  - B: open the command icon bar.
  - In the icon bar the D-pad moves the cursor and A runs the highlighted command and moves through messages.
  - The icons are Talk, Items, Look, Grab, Give and Hold. Hold is how you use an item.
  - Start: pauses the game. With the icon bar open, Start takes you to the Option menu, which shows your score, loads a saved game with a password and turns the music on or off.
  - On the password screen the D-pad moves the cursor, A enters a letter, and B cancels it. Move to END and press A when you are done.
- Saving
  - Ask an innkeeper for lodging and you get a password. Enter it on the title screen under Return to Times of Lore and you start in that inn with your items and your keywords. There is no battery.
  - The manual warns that keywords can be lost if you quit without saving. Forgetting to talk to someone can leave you stuck.
- Items from the manual
  - Red scroll: destroys every enemy on screen. Blue scroll: freezes them. Green scroll: takes you to a village of your choice, up to three times.
  - Blue potion: restores some health. Green potion: restores all of it. Red potion: lets you float and move fast.
  - Boots let you walk great distances. The dagger and axe are thrown, and the axe comes back to you.
  - You can only carry one of each scroll or potion at a time.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- The giant on the ridge
  - A giant in the mountains near Rhyder guards a magic ring that makes you invisible. The screen shakes when he moves
- The spy at the gate
  - To catch the spy you wait at the Ganestor gate at night and kill the guard who sneaks out. The spy looks like every other guard, so you may have to try a few. Touch the wrong guard and you land in jail. (Legacy of Games)
- Instant kills for the right item
  - The lyche dies the moment you use holy water on him, and the abbot dies the moment you use the sphere hidden under his bed. The final boss is a scavenger hunt. This is a game about finding the answer.
- The invisible ring and the blue scroll
  - Both can get you past guards. If you stand there trading blows with them, you are doing it the hard way.
- Pressure plates
  - Two dungeons are built around floor switches that have to be hit in the right order, and some are traps. In the desert temple, only one of three switches in a room is real.
- The manual's turnip soup
  - The manual shows a sample conversation. You say hello to a serf's wife, she mentions her husband is off fighting trolls, a bell rings, and you ask about treasure. She cannot help, so she offers you turnip soup. You say no thanks.
  - It also says the custom in Albareth is to greet people with a cheery "Nice weather we're having!" and that slaughtering peasants is a sure way to be ostracized.
- The ending
  - Kill the abbot, pick up a medallion and you are done. A string of story screens follows.

## V. History and Development

- The original
  - The working title was Ultra Realm. Roberts has said it was inspired by console action adventures, and The Legend of Zelda in particular. (Wikipedia)
  - It was a hit and won over critics. Computer Gaming World recommended it as a good first computer RPG because the menu interface made it easy to learn, and Compute! put it on a list of nine great games for 1989. (Wikipedia)
- The sibling
  - Bad Blood in 1990 used the same game system, with Roberts and Jeff George as designers. It is a post-apocalyptic action game rather than a fantasy one. Maybe more like Fallout?
- The port
  - The changes are all about speed and forgiveness. The NES adds shortcuts to the world map, makes townspeople harder to kill by accident, and has fewer lethal monsters and gentler pressure plates. The computer world is much larger, about sixteen times the size.
  - It plays smoothly with no real slowdown, even in big fights. (Legacy of Games)

## VI. Music and Presentation

- Graphics
  - Overhead view like early Zelda. One reviewer calls the graphics drab, and another says the NES cuts down the repeated scenery of the computer version. (Questicle, HonestGamers)
  - The candle in the corner is the nicest touch. It also doubles as a health bar and a hunger warning.
- Music
  - Seven tracks. The title, character select and ending are by Martin Galway, carried over from the computer versions. His title theme is considered one of his longest and best. (VGMPF, C64-Wiki)
  - Katsuhiro Hayashi wrote the overworld, chamber music and dungeon tracks, which exist only on the NES. VGMPF notes the NES version has background music during play, and the Option menu lets you turn it off.

## VII. Pop Culture

- Fantasy and role-playing in the late 1980s
  - Ultima was the big name in computer fantasy, and Origin made it. The Zelda games had just shown that an action game could carry a huge adventure. Times of Lore sits in the middle.
  - Neverwinter Nights, from AOL, SSI, TSR and Stormfront Studios, launched in March 1991, putting Dungeons & Dragons online. (Wikipedia)
  - Robin Hood: Prince of Thieves hit theaters June 14, 1991, took in about $390 million worldwide, and Bryan Adams spent 16 weeks at number one in the UK with its theme. A kingdom missing its king and a hero sent on errands fits right in. (Wikipedia, Official Charts)


## VIII. Sequels, Spinoffs and Comparison Games

- There was no sequel. These are the relatives.
- Ultima VI: The False Prophet (Origin, 1990)
  - Borrowed the icon interface and cut the command list to ten icons. It also added some real-time elements. (Wikipedia)
- Modern comparison games
  - Kingdom Come: Deliverance (Warhorse Studios, February 13, 2018, Windows, PlayStation 4, Xbox One)
    - A realistic medieval RPG set in 1403 Bohemia.
    - Both have NPCs with daily routines and a need to eat and sleep.
    - Kingdom Come is first person with a physics-based combat system and a classless progression. The NES game is an overhead action game with no progression at all.
    - The moment to point at is the opening, where Henry's village is raided, compared to your uncle quietly dying and you heading out to the Frothing Slosh.

## IX. Essential Games List

- Case for Essential
  - It is a quiet piece of design history. A talk-to-everyone adventure with picture menus, a day and night cycle and a candle for health, made in 1988 and cleaned up for the NES.
  - The password system is friendly, and the port runs smoothly with no slowdown.
- Case against Essential
  - Reviewers call it bland and easy. It earned a 4 out of 10 from a Famicom magazine, a C minus from Questicle and 3 out of 5 from HonestGamers.
  - It is too linear, the menus are stiff, and the key dialogue is easy to miss, so you can be stuck because you did not talk to the right person.
  - Combat is simple. You swing at enemies, and the final fights are won by finding the right object instead of skill.
- Suggested verdict: not essential, but a good curiosity for anyone who wants to see the roots of the Ultima icon interface and Chris Roberts before Wing Commander.
