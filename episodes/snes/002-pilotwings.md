# SNES 002 - Pilotwings

- "Are you ready to earn your wings at the most exclusive flight school in the world?"

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: a calm flight school where you earn pilot licenses one wobbly landing at a time
  - You are a rookie joining the Pilotwings Flight Club. You pass lessons in four very different aircraft, earn licenses, and climb the ranks.
- Who made it
  - Developed by Nintendo EAD, directed by Tadashi Sugiyama, produced by Shigeru Miyamoto. Music by Soyo Oka, with Koji Kondo handling sound programming and the helicopter theme. (Wikipedia)
  - Fun development stat: EAD shipped Pilotwings, Super Mario World, and F-Zero all within fifteen months of the SNES debut. (Wikipedia)
- The simplest way to frame it for listeners
  - This is not an action game. It is a serene, score-attack flying game about soaring through floating rings and then sticking the landing. Think driving test, but airborne, and weirdly relaxing.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- It exists to show off Mode 7
  - Mode 7 lets the SNES rotate and scale a flat image to fake a 3D ground plane underneath you. Pilotwings and F-Zero were the two games built specifically around that trick. (Hardcore Gaming 101, in its HyperZone piece)
  - There is no Super FX chip here. The buildings, runway, and trees are basically painted flat onto the ground, so they look like they pop up out of the floor when you are high above them. (Wikipedia)
- The tone is the actual hook
  - Most flight games of the era were combat: dogfights, missiles, After Burner energy. Pilotwings went the other way and made the appeal to the school of flying itself.
- Four totally different vehicles in one cartridge
  - Light Plane, Skydiving, Rocket Belt, and Hang Glider, each with its own controls and its own feel. (Wikipedia, GameFAQs)
- The weird-detail callout (our High Score Board moment): the instructors' faces
  - Your instructor reacts at the end of each event, and the faces get genuinely bizarre when you ace it. Shirley's eyes turn into flat vertical stick lines, Lance's eyes shrink and spin around inside their sockets, and stone-faced Big Al gets moved to actual tears. On an otherwise realistic-looking cast, it is delightfully strange. (StarBlog, TV Tropes)

## III. Gameplay Structure

- The loop
  - Four training areas, each with a few events. Fly through the markers, then land accurately inside a time limit. You are scored on completing the objective, the time taken, and the quality of the landing. Beat a combined score threshold to pass the area, and passwords let you save progress. (Wikipedia)
  - Eight main lessons in all, then secret "Expert" lessons that take place at night with higher winds and icy conditions. (Nintendo Life, Giant Bomb)
- Basic controls, vehicle by vehicle
  - Light Plane (biplane): limited roll and pitch, so no loops or barrel rolls, throttle to speed up or slow down, then land on the runway. Land too hard or too banked and you wreck it. (GameFAQs, ConsoleClassix)
  - Skydiving: jump from a helicopter, lean forward and back to steer, rotate on a horizontal axis, fall through rings of orbs, then pull the rip cord and drift down onto a bullseye target. (Wikipedia, GameFAQs)
  - Rocket Belt (jet pack): left and right yaw, lean forward and back for speed, B fires the jets (fast jets for speed, slow jets for fine control), and you have to watch your fuel. Tap L or R for an overhead view to line up the landing. (GameFAQs)
  - Hang Glider: cut loose from the tow plane, ride thermal currents up to a target altitude, A flares and quick-turns, then settle onto a gray square target. (GameFAQs, Wikipedia)
  - Press Start any time to see your position on the course from above, handy for hunting down the moving platform. (GameFAQs)
- The scoring layer
  - Hidden Bonus Chance stages unlock behind tricky feats like landing on a moving platform or dropping into the water target. They include guiding a diving penguin into a pool, bouncing a winged man across trampolines, and flinging another winged man as far as you can. The Light Plane is the one event with no bonus stage. (Wikipedia, GameFAQs)

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- Every vehicle is basically a different game
  - The skill does not transfer. Getting good with the biplane teaches you almost nothing about the hang glider, which is part of the charm and part of the frustration.
- Landing is the whole game
  - The flying is the easy part. Stopping cleanly, soft and centered, on the target is where the points live and where good runs go to die. (GameFAQs hint guides)
- The finicky bits, said honestly
  - Skydiving and rocket belt controls can feel fiddly, and the Lesson 3 hang glider climb (riding a thermal up to 500 feet) is a real disorienting difficulty spike. The game also does not hold your hand on the subtler control inputs, so your first landings feel like pure guesswork. (Nintendo Life)
- The mysterious instructor who never pays off
  - The manual builds Lance up as a man fluent in six languages with a secret Air Force past, and then none of that ever matters to anything in the game. It is a perfect NEStalgia "why is this here" detail. (TV Tropes)

## V. The Mode 7 Showpiece, and the Genre Shift Nobody Expects

- Where it came from
  - At the SNES reveal to the Japanese press in November 1988, Nintendo showed a flight-sim-style demo called Dragonfly purely to prove Mode 7 worked. Pilotwings is the direct descendant of that demo. (Wikipedia)
- The tonal whiplash ending
  - Clear all four areas and the calm evaporates. Your instructors Tony, Shirley, and Lance are kidnapped by the "EVIL Syndicate" on the fictional Izanu Island, and Big Al straps you into an attack helicopter off an offshore carrier to go get them back. (Wikipedia)
  - The helicopter mission has real teeth: ground turrets can shoot you down in a single hit, and there is no friendly retry banter afterward, you go straight to the game over screen. (Wikipedia, TV Tropes)
  - A later night mission sends you back out to rescue Big Al's brother. (TV Tropes)
  - Worth saying out loud on the show: a peaceful licensing sim quietly becomes a one-life military rescue. That is a wild swing for a cheerful Nintendo launch title.

## VI. Sequels and Spinoffs

- The series after this
  - Pilotwings 64 (1996), an N64 launch title built with Paradigm Simulation, finally delivered the dream in true 3D. (Wikipedia)
  - Pilotwings Resort (2011), a 3DS launch title from Monster Games, arrived after a long drought. (Wikipedia)
  - In between there was a long cancellation saga, including a Factor 5 GameCube version that migrated to the Wii and quietly died. (Wikipedia)
- One legacy oddity worth reading aloud
  - Roger Waters name-checked Pilotwings in a good-versus-evil dream sequence on "On the Run," from his 2023 album The Dark Side of the Moon Redux. A Pink Floyd founder referencing an SNES flight sim is peak show fodder. (Wikipedia)
- Comparison games
  - F-Zero: the sister launch title, the other game built to flaunt Mode 7, but fast and competitive where Pilotwings is calm.
  - Top Gun on the NES: the cautionary tale that landing a plane in a video game can be miserable. Pilotwings makes precision landing the entire point and somehow makes it feel good. (Nintendo Life draws this contrast.)
