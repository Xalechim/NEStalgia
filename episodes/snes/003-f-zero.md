# SNES 003 - F-Zero


## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: the fastest racing game anyone had seen at home in 1990, and the one that made the Super Nintendo look like it came from the future
  - It is the year 2560. Humanity has met aliens, interplanetary trade has made a lot of people very rich, and the rich want entertainment. The answer is Formula One style racing with machines that hover one foot above the track. The sport is called F-Zero.
  - You pick one of four pilots and one of four hovercraft, then race through the Knight, Queen and King Leagues for the title.
- Who made it
  - Developed by Nintendo EAD and published by Nintendo. Directed by Kazunobu Shimizu, produced by Shigeru Miyamoto, main programmer Yasunari Nishida, with Takaya Imamura designing the look of the cars and characters. Music by Yumiko Kanki and Naoto Ishida. (Wikipedia, Nintendo's 2017 developer interview)
  - A launch title: Japan on November 21, 1990, the same day as Super Mario World and Pilotwings
- What it grew out of
  - Nintendo had already made an overhead racer, Famicom Grand Prix: F1 Race, for the Famicom Disk System in 1987. F-Zero started as a follow up to it.
  - The look came from Tim Burton's 1989 Batman film. Shimizu bought Batman comics on a trip to America and brought them home as the mood board for a dark, futuristic city.
- The simplest way to frame it for listeners
  - A pure arcade racer with no weapons and no gimmicks. You, a field of rival machines, a track, and a speedometer pushing past 400 kilometers an hour.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- Mode 7 as a speed machine
  - The SNES could rotate and scale a flat background in hardware. F-Zero uses it to spin the track around your car, so a flat picture becomes a road rushing at you. No polygons.
- Hovercraft instead of cars
  - No wheels, no tires. That solved a technical problem (drawing turning tires from every angle would have multiplied the artwork) and gave the game its identity.
- One bar for health and the whole game
  - Your Power meter is your armor. It drains when you hit walls, mines or other racers, and a pit area near the home straight refills it. Getting hit is a resource decision, not just a penalty.
- A boost you earn every lap
  - Finish a lap and you earn a Super Jet, a boost of about four seconds. You can store up to three. Deciding when to cash them in is the strategy layer.
- Rank cutoffs that shove you forward
  - In a five lap race you must be 15th or better after lap one, 10th after lap two, 7th after lap three, 5th after lap four, and 3rd or better at the finish. Miss a cut and you are Ranked Out. Every lap the net gets tighter. (GameFAQs guides)
- Four machines with real personality
  - The Blue Falcon (Captain Falcon), Golden Fox (Dr. Stewart), Wild Goose (Pico) and Fire Stingray (Samurai Goroh). The in game specs: Fire Stingray tops out at 478 km/h and weighs 1,960 kg, the Wild Goose does 462 and weighs 1,620, the Blue Falcon does 457 and weighs 1,260, and the Golden Fox does 437 and weighs just 1,020. The Stingray is the fast, heavy, slow-accelerating king of time trials, the Goose is the tank, and the Fox is the light, quick-starting one. (GameFAQs guide, Nintendo Life)
- Fifteen tracks in three leagues, each with four difficulty levels
  - Beginner, Standard, Expert and Master, so the same tracks get meaner as you go. Master is a secret mode that only appears after you beat Expert in one league. Beat it and Captain Falcon himself tells you that you are an F-Zero Master.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- The loop
  - Choose Grand Prix or Practice. In Grand Prix you choose a league and a difficulty, pick a machine, and race every track in the league back to back. Five laps per race.
  - Every lap has a rank cutoff. Finish laps to earn Super Jets, and earn points for your place each lap: 900 for first, 600 for second, 400 for third and 200 for anything worse. Win the race for another 2,500 points, second gets 1,500 and third 1,000. You start with three lives, and every 10,000 points earns a spare machine. Run out and your championship is over. To win a league you must finish in the top three in all five races.
  - Practice mode lets you drive seven of the Grand Prix courses at your own pace, alone or with up to two rivals. Records are kept for the top ten times on all fifteen tracks, color coded by Grand Prix or Practice.
- The fifteen tracks
  - Knight League: Mute City I, Big Blue, Sand Ocean, Death Wind I, Silence.
  - Queen League: Mute City II, Port Town I, Red Canyon I, White Land I, White Land II.
  - King League: Mute City III, Death Wind II, Port Town II, Red Canyon II, Fire Field.
  - Several tracks are variations on each other. Mute City II is the first track with the city below turned orange and a big loop added, and Port Town I is a shortened Port Town II. Fire Field is the only track with no earlier version, and a full race on it takes around five minutes. (GameFAQs guide)
- Basic controls and handling
  - B is the throttle, A fires a Super Jet, and the D-pad steers. Hold L or R along with the D-pad to lean and slide harder into a corner. Hold Down in the air to fly farther and land without losing speed, or Up to cut the jump short. (GameFAQs guides)
  - Smooth, grippy handling is the whole reason the game works. Hairpins like the ones on Silence in the Knight League teach you how much to feather the turn.
- Hazards on the road
  - Mines, rough road, slip zones, and magnetic strips that drain your Power and pull you off line. Jump plates launch you off the track, and dash zones give you a free speed boost.
  - Slide off the edge and your machine explodes, so do not do that.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- The Rocket Start
  - Press the gas before the start signal and your engine backfires, your power drops, and rivals fly past you. That was the intended penalty. Players then found that a racer behind you can bump you forward, which turned the penalty into a trick: hold the gas and a shoulder button through the countdown, let go at the right moment, and a rival shoves you off the line. The team saw it before release and decided they liked it. (Nintendo's developer interview, GameFAQs guides)
- Mute City at day, evening and night
  - Mute City shows up three times with slightly different layouts. It was the most popular track with players, and its top time record stood for over a decade before it was finally broken.
- Hidden paths that open and close
  - On Death Wind, Port Town and Red Canyon a hidden pathway opens on one version of the course and closes the path you used before.
- The jump hang time trick
  - Staying in the air longer off a jump plate is actually faster than driving the same stretch. Hidden techniques like this are why speedrunners still care. (Nintendo Life)
- Death Wind and its crosswind
  - A steady crosswind pushes your machine from left to right on one straight and the other way on the next. Dash Zones on this track send you past 900 km/h. On Death Wind II some of them can throw you into the walls. (GameFAQs guide)
- The Port Town cheat that gets you caught
  - You can use a Super Jet and the right steering to jump the gap on Port Town II and skip the first corners. A floating UFO-like device then drags you back for cutting too much track. (GameFAQs guide)
- The Red Canyon II shortcut
  - A hidden jump plate sits in what looks like a wall. Hit it with a Super Jet, hold Down in the air, and a big blue arrow bounces you back onto the track ahead of everyone. The rivals never use it. You cannot do it on lap one because you do not have a Super Jet yet. (GameFAQs guide)
- Fire Field and the Master difficulty spike
  - Fire Field is the track people name when you ask where the game turns mean. The harder difficulties also bring aggressive computer racers that can feel like they rubber band back to you.
- The cover comic that ends as the race starts
  - The manual includes an eight page comic following Captain Falcon on a bounty mission, drawn in an American comic style by Imamura. It ends just as the race begins.
- Captain Falcon was not even a character yet
  - The pilots were an afterthought, decided when the game was nearly finished. Imamura says Captain Falcon actually began as a proposed mascot for the Super NES, a "Captain Something" designed in the red, blue and yellow of the controller buttons. His first sketch had him smoking a cigar.

## V. History and Development

- A tiny, in house team
  - Nine people made the game: three programmers, the artists who drew the backgrounds, Shimizu as director, and Miyamoto as producer. It took about a year and a half. (Nintendo's developer interview)
  - Nishida was the first programmer in the EAD department. Miyamoto had pointed out that Nintendo had outsourced most of its programming before the Super Famicom.
- How it started
  - Shimizu's team had made a sequel to Famicom Grand Prix: F1 Race and showed it to Nintendo of America. The reaction was cold. They said it was not a real racing game, racing cars should be cooler, and it would not sell. Shimizu was annoyed and decided to make something really cool.
  - Meanwhile Nishida was running a Mode 7 experiment, rotating the bottom four fifths of the screen to make a track and leaving the top fifth for a distant horizon. When Shimizu saw it he thought it would shock everyone.
- The first demo had big wheeled cars, like Hot Wheels toys
  - The wheels were dropped because tires turning at every angle would have required enormous amounts of pixel art. Removing them made the cars hover, and the future setting solved other problems too. A track floating in the sky over a city far below meant the team did not have to draw 3D buildings and their shadows.
- The Guard Beam
  - To show curves from every angle, the team lined the track edges with round objects that look the same from any direction. Shimizu named them the Guard Beam. A technical shortcut became the look of the game.
- The invisible wall that made the game worse
  - For much of development, an invisible wall stopped you from leaving the track. At Miyamoto's suggestion, the wall was removed. Imamura says that is what made F-Zero feel awesome, and it let the team add jump plates and shortcuts, with exploding machines for anyone who flies off.
- The boost
  - The boost was originally meant to blast through dirt. Players turned it into a way to get top times.
- Showing it off
  - At the Shoshinkai trade show before launch, Nintendo had about ten consoles running Super Mario World and two running F-Zero. Lines formed at the F-Zero machines. A young developer from another company asked Nishida if it was polygons. He said no.
- After the launch
  - A planned sequel on the SNES ended up as two Satellaview titles, BS F-Zero Grand Prix and BS F-Zero Grand Prix 2, broadcast in the mid 1990s on the St.GIGA service. (Wikipedia)

## VI. Music and Presentation

- Graphics
  - Bright, flat and clean. The tracks are bold colored ribbons, the Guard Beams give the edges a clear shape, and your machine is always readable. Nintendo Life calls the presentation "eye-blisteringly bright."
  - The honest limit: the tracks float over a distant city and there is very little scenery beside the road. The sense of speed carries it.
- Music
  - Fast, bright and tuneful, built for driving. Yumiko Kanki wrote the Mute City theme and Naoto Ishida wrote Big Blue. Death Wind's bass line is the one most reviewers single out.
  - The themes had a long life. A 12 track jazz arrangement album came out in Japan in March 1992, arranged by Robert and Michiko Hill with sax and guitar guest players. Mute City and Big Blue still show up in Super Smash Bros. and Mario Kart 8. (Wikipedia, Nintendo Life)
- Sound
  - The explosion when you leave the track was made as loud as possible, on purpose, for impact.

## VII. Pop Culture

- Batman, 1989
  - The dark, neon city skyline came straight out of Tim Burton's Batman, which was a cultural event in 1989. Shimizu was buying the comics while the film was a hit.
- The sport behind the game
  - Formula One was in a golden age. Ayrton Senna won the 1990 title in the same year F-Zero came out. The idea of a near-future Grand Prix was an easy sell.
- Hovering vehicles were the future
  - Back to the Future Part II put a hoverboard on every kid's wish list in 1989. A year later F-Zero let you drive something that hovered at 400 km/h.
- The launch
  - Nintendo's Super Famicom arrived in Japan on November 21, 1990, with two games, Super Mario World and F-Zero. One was the platformer, the other the speed demo.
- Captain Falcon after F-Zero
  - A character who barely existed became a star. In Super Smash Bros. for the Nintendo 64 (HAL Laboratory, 1999) he was one of four hidden fighters, along with Luigi, Ness and Jigglypuff. You earned him by clearing 1P mode in under 20 minutes and then beating him on Planet Zebes, because the game has no F-Zero stage. His look was taken from F-Zero X. He has been in every Smash game since, and the Falcon Punch is a meme in its own right. (SmashWiki, F-Zero Wiki)
  - Smash is the reason most people know Captain Falcon at all, and for a long time it was the only reason F-Zero stayed in the conversation. The series released no new game after 2004.
- The Mario Kart connection
  - Super Mario Kart (1992) began as a multiplayer F-Zero prototype. The team wanted two player racing and the characters came later. (Wikipedia)

## VIII. Sequels, Spinoffs and Comparison Games

- BS F-Zero Grand Prix (1996) and BS F-Zero Grand Prix 2 (1997)
  - Satellaview broadcast titles that started as the planned SNES sequel. The first added a new track and four vehicles, the second a new league of five tracks, and both kept the Grand Prix and Practice modes. (Wikipedia)
- F-Zero X (1998, Nintendo 64)
  - The first full sequel. It moved to true 3D, ran at 60 frames per second and put thirty machines on the track at once.
- F-Zero Maximum Velocity (2001, Game Boy Advance)
  - A launch title for the handheld that returned to the Mode 7 look of the original.
- F-Zero GX (2003, GameCube) and F-Zero AX (2003, arcade)
  - Developed with Amusement Vision, led by Toshihiro Nagoshi, who said the original "taught me what a game should be." GX is known as one of the hardest racing games ever released. (Wikipedia, Nintendo Life)
- F-Zero: GP Legend (2003, Game Boy Advance)
  - A story mode game tied to the anime of the same name.
- The anime: F-Zero: GP Legend (also known in Japan as F-Zero: Falcon Densetsu, 2003 to 2004)
  - A 51 episode series that ran on TV Tokyo from October 2003 to September 2004, tied to the Game Boy Advance game. Captain Falcon is voiced by Hideyuki Tanaka and the lead, Ryu Suzaku, by Toshiyuki Morikawa. (TMDB, MyAnimeList)
  - The English version came from 4Kids and aired on its FoxBox block, where the hero was renamed Rick Wheeler. It was cut short in the US. It is the series' only anime.
- F-Zero Climax (2004, Game Boy Advance)
  - Japan only. It is the last new F-Zero before a long silence.
- F-Zero 99 (2023, Nintendo Switch)
  - A free battle royale on Nintendo Switch Online that reuses the vehicles and courses from 1990, like Tetris 99. Its skyway boosts disappear the moment the bar runs out. (Wikipedia)
- Comparison games
  - WipEout (Psygnosis, 1995, PlayStation, Saturn and PC)
    - IGN credits F-Zero with inspiring the series. Futuristic anti-gravity racing, boost pads and a speed-first feel.
    - It differs because it adds weapons and a licensed electronic soundtrack, and is fully 3D.
    - Point to the soundtrack, which made racing feel like a club night.
  - Fast RMX (Shin'en Multimedia, 2017, Nintendo Switch)
    - A Switch launch title often compared directly to F-Zero. Its Hero mode uses the boost meter as the health bar, just like F-Zero's Power meter.
    - It differs because it is fully 3D and built around modern Switch features.
    - Point to the moment you choose between spending boost and saving it to stay alive.
  - Redout (34BigThings, 2016, Windows and later consoles)
    - Inspired by F-Zero, WipEout, Rollcage and POD. Anti-gravity racing with a heavy sense of speed.
    - It differs because it is a modern full 3D game with a career mode and vehicle upgrades.
    - Point to how it handles the same F-Zero problem of a track curving beneath you at insane speed.
  - Mario Kart 8 (Nintendo EAD, 2014, Wii U)
    - A direct relative: Mario Kart began as an F-Zero prototype. Mario Kart 8 later added the Blue Falcon kart and F-Zero tracks including Mute City and Big Blue as downloadable content.
    - It differs because it is about items, bumping and chaos rather than clean lines.
    - Point to driving Mute City upside down with anti-gravity wheels.

## IX. Essential Games List

- The case for
  - It is the cleanest example of a launch game doing exactly what a launch game should: show you what the new machine can do, in one minute, without a manual. And it is still fun to drive. The handling is tight, every mistake is yours, and a perfect lap feels like a trick you pulled off.
  - It created a genre. WipEout, Redout, Fast RMX and Mario Kart all owe it something.
- The case against
  - It is a single player game with no friend to race, which is a big part of why Super Mario Kart replaced it in most living rooms. The scenery is thin, the later difficulties lean on rubber band AI, and F-Zero X and GX are better games on nearly every measure.
  - The hardest part is not racing. It is the cut line rule that kicks you out of the Grand Prix if you slip one place on a single lap.
