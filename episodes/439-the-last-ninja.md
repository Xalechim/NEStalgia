# 439 - The Last Ninja

- Battle your way to a showdown with the evil shogun (Note for Claude: This is a tagline/sentence from the back of the box that works as intro for the show)

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: the last surviving ninja versus an evil shogun
  - You play as Armakuni, the last surviving member of the Brotherhood of the White Ninja.
  - The evil Shogun Kunitoki has slaughtered the brotherhood, taken over the Ninja Palace of Lin Fen, and stolen the sacred scrolls containing ancient ninja secrets.
- The NES version has an identity problem right away
  - Despite being called The Last Ninja, the NES game is really a port/adaptation of Last Ninja 2, which originally came from the Commodore 64.
  - That means the NES player is technically getting a sequel under the first game’s title.
  - Does it matter?
- Developed by Beam Software, published by Jaleco
- The simplest way to frame it for listeners
  - This is not Ninja Gaiden.
  - It is not a fast action-platformer.
  - It is an isometric action-adventure game about fighting enemies, solving puzzles, and trying to make your ninja stand in exactly the right spot.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The isometric presentation is the star
  - The goal is to make the world feel like a series of 3D spaces, with rooms, courtyards, rivers, doors, traps, and hidden interactable spots.
- It is more adventure game than action game
  - Yes, you fight enemies with punches, kicks, swords, staffs, nunchaku, and shuriken.
  - But the real game is often about figuring out what object you need, where to use it, and how to position yourself so the game understands what you are trying to do.
  - That makes it feel closer to a computer adventure game than a typical NES action game.
- It has a slow, deliberate pace
  - NES players expecting quick ninja action probably got something much stiffer and more methodical.
  - The game is about reading the environment, not blazing through it.
- The NES version is not just a simple port
  - Reported changes from the Commodore 64 version include simplified graphics, harder enemies, new layouts, missing or simplified areas, and easier item collection.
- High Score Board
  - The high score board starts with famous duos: Bert and Ernie, Barney and Fred, Stan Laurel and Oliver Hardy. Then it turns into‘HELLO MARKO AND REX.’ Whoever Marko and Rex are, they got immortalized right under Laurel and Hardy on one of the strangest scoreboards in the NES library.”

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Screen-by-screen exploration
  - You exit through the sides of the screen or through doors to move into another room or outdoor area.
  - Some areas allow backtracking, which matters because items often need to be carried to very specific places. (Take on the NES Library)
- Basic controls
  - D-pad: move in the isometric space.
  - A while standing still: punch or attack with the selected weapon.
  - B while standing still: kick, use an item, or pick up an item.
  - A while moving: jump.
  - Select + A: change items.
  - Select + B: change weapons.
  - Start: pause. (GameFAQs)
- Combat loop
  - Enemies appear on many screens and can block your path.
  - You can fight them, but fighting everyone is not always the smartest choice.
  - Because of the slow movement and stiff spacing, the game often rewards avoiding enemies or slipping past them more than trying to win every duel.
- Item and puzzle loop
  - You collect objects like keys, key cards, maps, food, bottles, meat, and code numbers.
  - Some items have obvious uses, like keys opening doors.
  - Others are more in depth, like needing altered food to deal with an animal blocking your path. (GameFAQs)

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- The fixed-distance jump
  - Jumping is not free and fluid.
  - It locks you into a fixed motion, so you need to line it up before committing.
  - This makes traps and water crossings feel tense, but not always in a good way.
- Item pickup precision
  - Picking up items requires standing close and pressing B.
  - The problem is that “close” is not always obvious in an isometric space.
- Combat has more options than it first appears
  - You can fight bare-handed or use weapons.
  - Weapons include things like staffs, swords, shuriken, nunchaku, and magic
- The map item is genuinely interesting
  - The map can flash clues for traps, unlock points, or items when entering a room.
  - But the flash is brief, so it is helpful without being generous.
- A game where the manual/walkthrough mindset matters
  - A player can get stuck because they missed a small object, failed to use an item in the exact spot, or did not understand what a screen was asking.

## VI. Sequels and Spinoffs

- The NES game sits inside a larger Last Ninja series
  - The main computer-game trilogy is:
    - The Last Ninja
    - Last Ninja 2
    - Last Ninja 3
- No true NES follow-up
  - There was no Last Ninja 2 or Last Ninja 3 released as separate NES follow-ups in North America.
  - For NES-only players, this was basically their one strange brush with the series.
- Comparison games
  - Solstice: useful comparison for isometric exploration and puzzle-like screen layouts.
  - Prince of Persia: not isometric, but similar in the sense that deliberate animation and exact positioning matter.

## VII. Essential Games List Vote

- Case against Essential
  - The controls are a major barrier.
  - The pacing is slow.
  - The item interaction can be frustratingly precise.
  - The NES version is probably not the best way to understand why people loved The Last Ninja series.
  - A game can be historically interesting without being something every NES player needs to experience.
