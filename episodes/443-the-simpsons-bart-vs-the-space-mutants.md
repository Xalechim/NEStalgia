- 1. The premise and its cult movie connection
 Bart finds X-ray specs in the back of a comic book and discovers aliens have replaced half of Springfield. The specs are a direct lift from John Carpenter's They Live, where Roddy Piper's sunglasses do the same trick. Surprising for a kids' game in 1991.
- 2. Who made it and when it landed
 Developed by Imagineering, published by Acclaim, designed by Garry Kitchen (the Battle Tank guy). This is the first Simpsons video game ever, released February 1991 at the height of Bartmania.
- 3. Why it's not the game people are thinking of
 This isn't the beloved Konami arcade beat-em-up. Different developer, different genre, and it actually came first. Good to clear this up early since most casual fans conflate the two.
- 4. Scavenger hunt disguised as a platformer
 Instead of racing to the end of a level, Bart combs each screen for things to grab, spray, or knock loose. Level one leans almost into point-and-click adventure territory, which is the part people remember fondly.
- 5. The five Springfield levels and their gimmick
 Streets, Mall, Krustyland, Natural History Museum, Nuclear Power Plant. Each level fixates on one target item (purple objects, hats, balloons, exit signs, nuclear rods) that you need to hit a quota on before leaving.
- 6. The family-help mechanic
 Beating disguised mutants drops letters. Spell a family member's name and they show up to help with the boss fight. A clever license-flavored twist on "saving the world."
- 7. Control quirks worth flagging
 Tap-then-hold A to run (B is busy spraying paint and firing weapons), which trips up anyone raised on Mario's hold-B-to-run habit. A+B together gives a long jump you'll need later.
- 8. The flaws that get worse after level one
 Fake 3D depth platforms are wildly inconsistent about what's actually solid ("depth perplexion"). Puzzle logic gets obscure (buy a wrench, use it on a fire hydrant, no real hint). You also lose every tool at the end of each level, so there's no sense of building power.
- 9. High Score Board trivia
 The payphone prank call gag in level one, where Bart can call Moe's Tavern asking for I. M. Adope, Stu Piddiddiot, and Isabelle Ringing. Runner-up: shooting the "E" in the Kwik-E-Mart sign for three extra lives and a hidden sound test.
- 10. The rushed development and surprising commercial success
 Acclaim wanted it out for Christmas 1990, production started in May 1990, and it missed the deadline anyway, shipping early 1991. Kitchen has said the later levels suffered from the rush. Despite mixed reviews, it sold over a million copies and became an NES best-seller.

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch in one line
  - Bart gets X-ray glasses out of the back of a comic book, and instead of seeing through clothes he sees that half of Springfield has been replaced by aliens.
  - He is the only one who knows, and he has to wreck their invasion one purple object at a time.
- The story
  - The Space Mutants are building an "ultimate weapon" and need specific items to fuel it.
  - Each level they fixate on one thing: purple objects, then hats, balloons, exit signs, and finally nuclear rods.
  - Bart's job is to collect or destroy those items before they can grab them.
- Who made it
  - Developed by Imagineering, published by Acclaim, designed by Garry Kitchen (REMEMBER HIM?! Battle Tank).
  - This is the first ever Simpsons video game, February 1991, riding the absolute peak of Bartmania.
- The simplest way to frame it for listeners
  - This is not the Konami arcade beat-em-up that everybody loves. Different game, different company, came first.
  - It is a slow, puzzle-flavored scavenger-hunt platformer. Closer to "find every purple thing on the screen" than "punch your way to the boss."

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The They Live premise is the whole hook
  - The X-ray specs that reveal aliens hiding in human bodies are a straight lift from John Carpenter's They Live, where Roddy Piper's sunglasses do the same trick. Hard to believe that made it into a kids' game in 1991, but here we are.
- It is a scavenger hunt wearing a platformer's clothes
  - Most NES platformers are about getting to the end. This one is about combing each screen for things to grab, spray, or knock loose.
  - The first level genuinely has a pinch of point-and-click adventure in it, which is what people remember fondly (1UP/Bob Mackey).
- Early-Simpsons weirdness baked right in
  - The villains are the Space Mutants, a throwaway alien gag from the show's earliest seasons. If this game were made later, it would star Homer and the bad guys would be Kang and Kodos. It is a time capsule of season-one Simpsons (TV Tropes).
  - The devs had no pre-release material, so they watched episodes as they aired and crammed in whatever references they could catch (Retro Gamer, "The Making Of").
- High Score Board
  - The marquee weird detail here is the payphone. In level one Bart can prank-call Moe's Tavern asking for fake customers, and the names are pure Simpsons: I. M. Adope, Stu Piddiddiot, Isabelle Ringing. Acclaim did not have to put that in, and the fact that they did is the most charming thing in the game.
  - Runner-up: fire a rocket at the "E" in the Kwik-E-Mart sign and you get three extra lives plus a hidden sound test (GameFAQs). One of the better-known NES easter eggs of the era.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Five themed Springfield levels
  - The streets of Springfield, the Springfield Mall, Krustyland, the Natural History Museum, and the Nuclear Power Plant.
  - Each level gives you a target item type and a quota to hit before the exit.
- The controls have one quirk worth flagging
  - D-pad moves, A jumps, and you tap-then-hold A to run, because B is busy spraying paint and firing weapons (Neoseeker). Anyone raised on Mario holding B to run will fight this for a while.
  - Press A and B together for a long jump, which you will need for the meaner gaps later on.
- The family-help mechanic
  - Beat disguised mutants and you collect letters. Spell out a family member's first name and that Simpson shows up to help you in the boss fight (Wikipedia).
  - It is a neat license-flavored idea: you are not just saving the world, you are convincing your own family you are not lying.
- The coin economy
  - Jumped-on aliens drop coins ("proof"), which you spend on tools like rockets and on the Krustyland mini-games.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- "Get rid of everything purple" is a genuinely fun design idea
  - You are not just collecting, you are also neutralizing. Spray a purple poster red, knock a hat off someone's head, prank Moe so he runs outside and you can paint his apron.
  - When a solution clicks, it feels great. That is the part defenders defend.
- The fake-depth platforms are a real gotcha
  - The game fakes 3D by letting you "walk" along rivers or stand on the horizon by jumping onto the background. It is wildly inconsistent about which background is solid (TV Tropes calls it "depth perplexion").
- A lot of the puzzle logic is obscure
  - Buy a wrench, use it on a fire hydrant. Use a whistle, but only in the exact right spot or a dog mauls you. This is a game that quietly expects you to have a guide.
- Be honest about the flaws
  - Level one is the most thought-out by a mile. The difficulty spikes hard afterward and the later stages get genuinely punishing and less reference-packed (Mackey; Sega-16).
  - Bart slides on his landings and the jump is a little jerky. Not broken, but stiff (Sega-16 argues the "worst controls ever" reputation is overblown, for the record).
  - You drop every tool you collected at the end of each level, so there is no sense of building up power (TV Tropes, "Bag of Spilling").

## V. History, Development & Reception

- Built fast against a fixed ship date
  - Acclaim wanted it out for Christmas 1990. Full production started in May 1990 and missed the deadline, shipping early 1991 (Retro Gamer).
  - Kitchen has openly said the game's quality suffered from rushing to a fixed date, and that the later levels needed more tuning he never got time for (Wikipedia, citing the Hickey interview).
- A monster commercial hit anyway
  - Reviews were mixed, with the controls and difficulty taking the hits, but it sold over a million copies and became an NES best-seller (Wikipedia).
  - Contemporary reviews split along fan lines: Crash and Your Sinclair loved it, Nintendo Power warned it was frustratingly hard, Entertainment Weekly gave it a B and praised the puzzle-over-reflex design (Lou Kesten).

## VI. Sequels and Spinoffs

- Where it sits in the Simpsons-game family
  - Followed the very same year by The Simpsons: Bart vs. the World on NES, basically the same engine with a globe-trotting coat of paint.
  - Then a wave of Bart games: Bart's Nightmare, Virtual Bart, Krusty's Fun House, all the way up to the much later The Simpsons Game.
- The port story is its own wrinkle
  - The NES version is the original. Everything else (Genesis, Master System, Game Gear, the home computers) was ported afterward by Arc Developments.
  - There was reportedly an isometric Simpsons game in development for home computers that got scrapped in favor of this conversion, and at least one reviewer thought it looked more interesting (MobyGames Amiga review).
  - The Genesis version quietly dropped the Simpsons theme song that the NES version proudly included, and lost some references to licensing issues (Sega-16).
- Comparison games
  - The Simpsons (Konami arcade): the obvious contrast. Same license, far more beloved, completely different genre. Good debate fuel for "why did the brawler age better."
  - Hudson Hawk or other Acclaim license platformers: useful for placing this in the early-90s licensed-platformer boom.
  - Any point-and-click adventure: worth raising because level one's "use the right item in the right spot" loop is closer to that than to Mario.

## VII. Essential Games List Vote

- Case for Essential
  - It is the first Simpsons game ever and a million-selling cultural artifact of peak Bartmania, and that first level remains a legitimately clever, reference-stuffed piece of design. If the list is about NES history and not just the best-playing carts, it has a real claim.
- Case against Essential
  - The game falls apart after level one, sliding into stiff, obscure, punishing platforming that most kids never pushed past, so what you actually remember fondly is about one-fifth of the cartridge.
