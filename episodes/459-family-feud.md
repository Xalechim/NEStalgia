# 459 - Family Feud

- It's time to play the Feud! Can you take the pressure? The box goes on to promise all the lightning-fast action of one of television's most popular family game shows. (VGCollect box listing)

## I. Setup & Premise

- What it is based on: the TV game show Family Feud
  - Two families of five face off. Each question was put to 100 surveyed people, and every answer on the board is worth points based on how many of them said it. (Wikipedia)
  - Three wrong answers is three strikes, and the other family gets a chance to steal.
  - The winning family goes on to the Fast Money bonus round for a top prize of $5,000. (Wikipedia)
- Who made it
  - Beam Software developed it, out of Melbourne, Australia. GameTek published it in North America in May 1991, and it was the first Family Feud game GameTek published. (Giant Bomb)
  - GameTek was the company that turned game shows into a product line on the NES: Jeopardy! and Wheel of Fortune in September 1988, Hollywood Squares in 1989, and more.
- The pitch
  - You and your family of five against a computer family, or against a friend's family.
  - The box sells "real surveys from real shows." It also says each family member gets 45 seconds to come up with an answer before the winners race the clock in Fast Money. (box text)

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- You spell your answers out
  - There is no multiple choice. You scroll through the alphabet with the controller and spell each answer, with a time limit. (Wikipedia)
- The game judges your answer
  - It accepts reasonable synonyms and slight misspellings, so you do not have to hit the survey wording exactly. (Wikipedia)
- A host who flirts
  - The host sprite kisses the female contestants, a nod to the Dawson-era show. (Wikipedia)
- A computer family you can lose to
  - Play one-player and you face a computer-controlled family. Per the manual, your family can win up to five games, but once the computer family wins, you start over.
- A real goal to chase
  - The game keeps score in dollars, and a family that goes past $20,000 retires as champions. (Wikipedia)
- Two-family play
  - A friend can run the other family. (Wikipedia, Just Games Retro)

## III. Gameplay Structure

- The core loop
  - Each question has a board of answers ranked by how many of the 100 people gave them. Five family members get a chance to answer. (manual)
  - Name something that is not on the board and you take a strike. Three strikes and the other family can steal your points with one correct answer.
  - You play two or more main rounds to build up dollars, then go for the $5,000 in Fast Money. (manual)
  - Fast Money is a run of five questions. (TASVideos)
  - Fall short in Fast Money and the game still pays you $5 for every point you scored. (Wikipedia)
- The basic controls
  - D-pad: moves the cursor through the letters to spell your answer, and you can wrap around the ends of the alphabet. (manual, Wikipedia)
  - A button: locks in each letter. You use the same method to type your family name at the start. (manual)
- The question pool
  - More than 400 questions, each with 3 to 12 answers on the board. (TASVideos)

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- How answers match
  - The synonym system is hit and miss. One reviewer found that "looks" counted for "appearance" while "clothing" earned a strike for "clothes." (Just Games Retro)
  - Players hunting achievements call the rejected-answer technicalities absurd. (RetroAchievements)
- You cannot see what the other family said
  - You only learn whether their answer was right or a strike. (Just Games Retro)
- The computer family and the tax man
  - When a computer family wins, Fast Money is skipped and the game gives an excuse such as "tax considerations" or "a birth in the family." It is carried over from the ShareData versions. (Wikipedia)
- What happens at the top
  - The game tells you that you win at $20,000, and the payoff is a title screen congratulating you. The reviewer calls it a gimmick to keep you playing. (Just Games Retro)
  - There is even an achievement for earning $20,000 and then being removed from the show. (RetroAchievements)
- A dated survey
  - The answers come from the 1980s survey era, and some feel like a time capsule. (RetroAchievements)
- Repetition
  - Play long enough and the questions repeat. (Just Games Retro)

## V. History and Development

- Beam Software
  - Founded in Melbourne in 1980 by Alfred Milgrom and Naomi Besen. The name combines their surnames. It began making books and software for Melbourne House, a publisher the founders started in London in 1977. (Wikipedia)
  - It got a Nintendo developer license in 1987. Its NES work includes Back to the Future (1989), The Punisher (1990), Smash TV (1991), and Star Wars (1991). (Wikipedia)
  - It operated independently from 1987 to 1999, was bought by Krome Studios in November 2006, and closed on October 15, 2010. (Wikipedia)
- GameTek
  - Started as a trade name for I.J.E., a children's entertainment distributor founded in 1977 that held TV licenses. Founder Irwin Schwartz formed Gabco in 1987 to buy I.J.E. back, and GameTek was created to turn those licenses into software. (Wikipedia)
  - Its game-show list included Wheel of Fortune, Jeopardy!, Family Feud, The Price Is Right, Hollywood Squares, Double Dare, and Classic Concentration. (Wikipedia)
  - It filed for Chapter 11 in December 1997 and went out of business in July 1998. (Wikipedia)
- Where this game came from
  - The first Family Feud video game was ShareData's 1987 release on Apple II and Commodore 64. The All New Family Feud followed in 1989, based on the Ray Combs era. (Wikipedia)
- Credits
  - Wikipedia credits Tania Smith with the music.

## VI. Music and Presentation

- Graphics
  - The family portraits are cartoon-style and stereotyped, and the host walks down the line of them while you enter your family name. (Just Games Retro)
  - The host himself is rough. One reviewer says he looks like a bad drawing of Elvis, and the kissing is what tells you he is meant to be Richard Dawson. (Just Games Retro)
  - The box shows the Ray Combs set, but the game recreates the look of the Dawson show. (Wikipedia)
- Music
  - The opening theme is an odd mix of rockabilly and electronica. A few seconds faintly resemble the real theme and the rest does not. (Just Games Retro)
  - The real theme was written by Walt Levinsky, Ken Bichel, and Robert Israel for Score Productions. (Wikipedia)
  - The box promises upbeat music and contestants who cheer when they win and look dejected when they lose.

## VII. Pop Culture

- The TV show's history
  - Mark Goodson built Family Feud out of the Match Game boom. Early working titles were Odd Ball and Fast Company. (Wikipedia)
  - It premiered on ABC on July 12, 1976, replacing Rhyme and Reason, with Richard Dawson as host. A syndicated nighttime version began September 19, 1977. (Wikipedia)
  - Dawson won the Daytime Emmy for game show host in 1978. (Wikipedia)
  - He started kissing contestants after he saw a nervous woman's hands shaking and told her it was not open heart surgery. (Mental Floss, Looper)
  - Wheel of Fortune passed Family Feud as the top daytime game show in 1984. The ABC run ended June 14, 1985. (Wikipedia)
- The Combs era, the show behind this game
  - CBS daytime premiered July 4, 1988 with Ray Combs. The syndicated version began September 19, 1988. (Wikipedia)
  - In fall 1991, Family Feud and The Price Is Right were the only game shows left on CBS daytime. (Wikipedia)
  - On June 29, 1992, CBS expanded to an hour, added the Bullseye round, and called it Family Feud Challenge. The CBS show aired its last episode March 26, 1993. (Wikipedia)
  - Dawson returned in 1994, and the show ended in May 1995. (Wikipedia)
- The 1991 game-show landscape
  - The $100,000 Pyramid returned on January 7, Studs debuted March 11, and The Chuck Woolery Show debuted on NBC September 9. (Wikipedia)
- Game-show video games
  - GameTek and Rare released Hollywood Squares in September 1989 and Super Jeopardy! on September 1, 1991. (Giant Bomb)
  - The SNES Jeopardy! followed in December 1992 with digitized footage of Alex Trebek. (LaunchBox)
- Beyond TV and video games
  - Milton Bradley issued the board game in 1977, Pressman put out editions in 1990 and 1993, and Endless Games released a 40th anniversary edition in 2017. (Wikipedia, collector listings)
  - An early computer version came out on the Coleco Adam in 1983. (Wikipedia)
  - The UK version, Family Fortunes, began in 1980 with Bob Monkhouse, who suggested the name because "feud" sounded too aggressive. The show has more than 50 international versions. (Wikipedia)
- The later TV era
  - Louie Anderson hosted the revival from September 20, 1999. Richard Karn followed in 2002 and John O'Hurley in 2006. (Wikipedia)
  - Steve Harvey took over July 10, 2010, and in June 2015 the show passed Wheel of Fortune as the most-watched syndicated game show. (Wikipedia)
  - Celebrity Family Feud began with a short-lived NBC edition in 2008 hosted by Al Roker, and ABC revived it in 2015 with Harvey. (Wikipedia)
- What else was going on in 1991
  - Operation Desert Storm began January 17. The Silence of the Lambs opened February 14. (Wikipedia, The Numbers)
  - Sonic the Hedgehog hit the Genesis June 23, and Terminator 2 opened wide July 2. (Wikipedia, The Numbers)
  - The Super NES began shipping August 23 at $199, with a nationwide release September 9. (Wikipedia)
  - Nicktoons Doug, Rugrats, and The Ren & Stimpy Show launched August 11. Home Improvement debuted on ABC September 17, and Nirvana's Nevermind came out September 24. (Wikipedia)

## VIII. Sequels, Spinoffs and Comparison Games

- Earlier computer games
  - ShareData Family Feud, 1987 (Apple II, Commodore 64)
    - The first Family Feud video game.
  - The All New Family Feud, 1989 (PC, Apple, C64)
    - Updated to the Combs-era rules.
- The GameTek series
  - Super NES, 1993, Imagineering
    - Moves to the Combs set and adds the Bullseye round and a returning-champs password. (Just Games Retro)
    - Answer matching is still rigid, and only four families are available. (Just Games Retro)
  - Genesis, 1993, Eurocom
    - Uses the Combs set. One review says the families look barely detailed. (Wikipedia, Sega-16)
  - 3DO, 1994, Eurocom
    - A voiced host, a first for the series, plus Bullseye and up to ten players. (TCRF)
  - MS-DOS, 1995 (Wikipedia)
- Later series
  - Family Feud, 2000, Artech Studios and Hasbro Interactive (PlayStation, PC)
    - Full-motion video of Louie Anderson and more than 1,500 surveys. The PC version lets you put your own face on a 3D body. (PSX Data Center)
  - Family Feud, 2003 and 2008 (mobile)
    - Phone versions from Mobliss and Glu Mobile. (Wikipedia)
  - Family Feud, 2006, Global Star Software (PS2, GBA, PC)
    - The PS2 version added online play and sold for $19.99. (press release)
  - Family Feud 2010 Edition, Ludia and Ubisoft (Wii, DS, PC)
    - Uses the 2009 set, with no host and a voiceover. (Wikipedia)
  - Family Feud Decades, Ubisoft, November 2010 (Wii)
    - Lets you play on sets from different eras of the show. Nintendo Life said it changed little from the earlier game.
  - Family Feud 2012 Edition, Ubisoft (Wii, Xbox 360) (Wikipedia)
  - Family Feud, Snap Finger Click and Ubisoft, November 2020 (PS4, Xbox One, Switch)
    - Reach 300 points across four rounds to unlock Fast Money. Reviewers called it a pretty good party game with little that impresses. (The Xbox Hub)
- Other GameTek game-show games
  - Jeopardy! and Wheel of Fortune (1988), Wheel of Fortune Junior Edition (1989), Hollywood Squares (1989), Super Jeopardy! (1991). (Giant Bomb)
- Modern comparison games
  - The Jackbox Survey Scramble (2024)
    - The closest cousin. Real survey answers, guess the most and least popular, 2 to 10 players on phones. One review calls it Family Feud meets Jackbox silliness. (Nintendo Life)
  - Quiplash (Jackbox, June 30, 2015)
    - Not a survey game, but the other half of the idea: you type your answers and the room judges them. (Giant Bomb)
  - Trivia Crack (2013)
    - Mobile trivia that passed 500 million downloads, the other way to make a quiz social. (PocketGamer.biz)
  - America's Greatest Game Shows: Wheel of Fortune and Jeopardy! (Ubisoft, 2020)
    - The modern version of GameTek's lineup, with family modes and online play. (Ubisoft)
  - Mario Party Superstars (October 29, 2021)
    - A game built for a room full of people, which is the same reason Family Feud works at all. (reviews)

## IX. Essential Games List

- Case for Essential
  - It tries to make a living room argue about what 100 strangers said, which almost no NES game attempts.
  - Typing answers letter by letter and having the game accept synonyms is a genuine ambition for 1991.
  - It is cheap. A loose cartridge runs about $11. (VGCollect, PriceCharting)
  - It rewards fans of the show, and the Dawson lookalike gives it personality.
- Case against Essential
  - Spelling every answer with a D-pad is slow, and the matching punishes reasonable answers.
  - Many survey answers are decades old and feel arbitrary.
  - Most of the fun needs people in the room, and a solo player gets only a computer family.
  - One review rates it 2 out of 5. (Just Games Retro)
- Suggested verdict: not essential, but a fun curiosity for anyone who wants to see how GameTek put a TV show on a cartridge.
