# 459 - Family Feud

- It's time to play the Feud! Can you take the pressure? (Note: this is the opening of the back-of-box copy, which goes on to promise "all the lightning-fast action of one of television's most popular family game shows." Source: VGCollect box listing)

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: you and your "family" of five face the computer, or a second family, in the TV game show
  - Two families of five answer survey questions. Each answer is ranked by how many of 100 surveyed people gave it. (Wikipedia, Just Games Retro)
  - The box says each family member has 45 seconds to come up with an answer, then the winners race the clock in the Fast Money Bonus Round. (VGCollect box text)
  - The box also sells "real surveys from real shows" and the promise of "easy to learn, easy to play, challenging to win." (box text as quoted in listings)
- Which game is this? Sorting out the dates
  - The series began with ShareData in 1987 on Apple II and Commodore 64, and a 1989 ShareData release, The All New Family Feud, on PC, Apple, and C64 that followed the Ray Combs era format. (Wikipedia, Family Feud video game series)
  - The NES version is the first game GameTek published in the series. Beam Software developed it. (Wikipedia)
  - Source conflict: the Wikipedia series intro says GameTek put out an NES version in 1990, while its detailed section and every other source we found say May 1991 (Wikipedia, VGCollect). A few sites give May 1, 1991. We found no evidence of a separate 1990 NES game, so treat 1990 as an error.
  - Cart code NES-YF-USA, published by GameTek, North America. We did not turn up a Japan or Europe release. (Gameventory)
- Who made it
  - Beam Software, Melbourne, Australia. Wikipedia credits Tania Smith with the audio. Beam is now part of the Krome Studios story (see Section VII).

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- It tries to be the TV show on a cartridge
  - The box promises contestants who cheer when they win and look dejected when they lose, plus upbeat music. (box text)
  - A host sprite kisses the female contestants. Wikipedia and a retro review say he is clearly meant to be Richard Dawson. (Wikipedia, Just Games Retro)
  - Worth noting: in 1991 the real show was hosted by Ray Combs, not Dawson. The game apparently borrowed the older, more famous host's look and habits. (Wikipedia: Family Feud)
- You type your answers
  - Wikipedia says you enter answers by scrolling through the alphabet with the controller.
  - It accepts reasonable synonyms and slight misspellings. (Wikipedia)
  - A retro reviewer found the matching inconsistent: "looks" counts for "appearance" but "clothing" does not count for "clothes." (Just Games Retro)
- Discussion question: how does a game show survive being a video game?
  - The show is about guessing what strangers said. The game has to be its own judge of what counts as a match. Is a typed-in answer system fair?

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Menu options
  - Choose 1 Family, 2 Family, or Practice, then press Start. (RetroAchievements, manual excerpts)
  - One-player means you play a computer family. Two-player means two humans trade off. (Wikipedia, Just Games Retro)
- Rounds and rules (sources disagree on numbers)
  - Five family members each get a question and the board of top answers.
  - Three wrong answers is three strikes, and the other family gets a chance to steal. (Just Games Retro)
  - Just Games Retro says the goal is 200 points over three rounds. Wikipedia says the winning threshold is $200. One summary of the manual excerpts says 300 points. We could not read the manual ourselves, so confirm before saying a number on air.
- Fast Money
  - The winning family plays Fast Money. Wikipedia lists a $5,000 top prize for 200 or more points, which matches the TV show's CBS daytime Fast Money top prize. (Wikipedia: Family Feud)
  - RetroAchievements and manual excerpts describe five questions in 20 seconds, then the same five asked again for 25 seconds with no repeated answers.
  - The Fast Money winnings carry into a running total. Reaching 20,000 dollars triggers an ending, which the reviewer describes as a title screen congratulating you. (Just Games Retro)

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- Answer entry
  - Spelling the answer out letter by letter with a gamepad is a bigger chore than yelling it on TV. How long does a round take?
- A computer family with opinions
  - The reviewer says the CPU family has several odd reasons not to play Fast Money. (Just Games Retro)
- You can't see what the other family said
  - The reviewer complains that you can't see opponent answers, which makes strategy hard. (Just Games Retro)
- Repetition
  - The reviewer noted a limited question pool and repeats over long sessions. Compare that to the ending target: roughly 20,000 dollars means a lot of rounds.
  - A search summary mentioned "over 4,000 questions," but it was attached to the Super NES and Genesis versions (and to Ray Combs and Bullseye, which are not in the NES game), so we are not claiming it for the NES.
- No passwords or cheats turned up
  - We found no NES password list. The Super NES version has password cheats; the NES one apparently does not. (unverified)

## V. Pop Culture: The Show and Game Shows in 1991

- The TV show in May 1991
  - The original run was 1976 to 1985, on ABC and in syndication, hosted by Richard Dawson. (Wikipedia)
  - Revived in 1988: CBS daytime, premiering July 4, 1988 with Ray Combs, and a syndicated version beginning September 19, 1988. (Wikipedia)
  - In 1991, it ran at 9 AM on CBS, followed by Wheel of Fortune at 9:30 and The Price Is Right at 10. (Wikipedia, Ray Combs)
  - Dawson's trademark: kissing female contestants, which started in 1976 as a way to calm a nervous contestant ("it's not open heart surgery"). (Looper, Mental Floss search results)
  - Later changes: June 29, 1992, CBS expanded to an hour and added the Bullseye round, retitled Family Feud Challenge. The CBS version ended March 26, 1993. Dawson returned to host before the syndicated run ended in 1995. (Wikipedia)
- Other game-show video games
  - GameTek's list: Jeopardy! (September 1988), Wheel of Fortune (September 1988), Wheel of Fortune Junior Edition (1989), Hollywood Squares (1989), Double Dare (1990), Wheel of Fortune Family Edition (1990), Super Jeopardy! (September 1, 1991). (Giant Bomb, Wikipedia, search results)
  - Many were developed by Rare. (search results)
- What else was happening
  - The Silence of the Lambs came out February 14, 1991 and won the big five Oscars. Terminator 2 hit in July. (search results)
  - Super NES launched in North America August 23, 1991, three months after this game. (SNES Central)
- Discussion question
  - The show is a living-room experience. Is a game show a good fit for a console that was mostly about jumping and shooting?

## VI. Reception & Legacy

- Contemporary coverage: we could not find a Nintendo Power, EGM, or GamePro review of the NES game.
- Retro take: Just Games Retro gives it 2 out of 5. It calls it a "pretty solid recreation of the show," but says it gets boring long before you make the twenty grand.
- Collectibility: loose cart about $11 (VGCollect via PriceCharting). It is cheap and common. A complete copy came with the registration card, epilepsy warning sheet, and Nintendo Power card. (VGCollect)
- Legacy: it launched GameTek's run of Family Feud games.

## VII. Sequels and Spinoffs

- The GameTek series
  - 1993: Super NES (Imagineering) and Genesis (Eurocom), October 1993. 1994: 3DO (Eurocom). 1995: MS-DOS. (Wikipedia)
  - Genesis reviews: EGM gave it 70 and GamePro 83. (Sega Retro via search results)
  - Later publishers: Hasbro Interactive, Global Star, and Ubisoft starting in 2000. (Wikipedia)
- GameTek the company
  - Grew out of I.J.E., founded 1977 by Jerry Weiner and Irwin Schwartz. It held game show licenses: Wheel of Fortune, Jeopardy!, Family Feud, The Price Is Right, Hollywood Squares, Tic-Tac-Dough. Filed for Chapter 11 on December 3, 1997 and shut down in July 1998. (Wikipedia: GameTek)
- Beam Software
  - Founded 1980 by Alfred Milgrom and Naomi Besen. Independent from 1987 to 1999, then Infogrames Melbourne House, sold to Krome Studios in 2006, closed October 15, 2010. (Wikipedia)
  - Other Beam NES work: Back to the Future, The Punisher, Battle Chess, The Hunt for Red October, Smash TV, Star Wars, Aussie Rules Footy. Super NES: Shadowrun (1993) and MechWarrior. (Wikipedia)

## VIII. Essential Games List Vote

- Case for Essential
  - It does what it says: a recognizable show with a host, a board, strikes, steals, and Fast Money.
  - Two-family play is a decent party hook, and the 45-second family format is easy to understand.
  - Typed answers with synonym recognition were ambitious for a 1991 NES cart.
- Case against Essential
  - Limited question pool and repetition.
  - Inconsistent answer matching and a clunky letter-by-letter entry.
  - The host is a Dawson lookalike while the actual 1991 show had Combs.
  - It is a licensed adaptation that is more a curiosity than an essential. Retro reviewers land around 2 out of 5.
