## I. Setup & Premise

- What it is based on: Parker Brothers' Monopoly board game  
  - Charles Darrow of Germantown, Pennsylvania made the first sets by hand and sold a few through a Philadelphia department store. Parker Brothers acquired the rights in 1935\.&nbsp;  
  - The idea is older. Elizabeth "Lizzie" Magie patented The Landlord's Game on January 5, 1904, as a lesson against land monopolies. It featured two rule sets that players could switch between: a "Monopolist" set (where players tried to crush each other) and an "Anti-Monopolist" or "Prosperity" set (where building property benefited everyone collectively and rent was shared).  
  - The board is Atlantic City: 40 spaces, with 22 streets in eight color groups, four railroads and two utilities. Marvin Gardens is really Marven Gardens, in Margate, and Parker Brothers did not apologize for the spelling until 1995\.  
  - The rules, EVERYONE HAS THEIR OWN: \$1,500 to start, \$200 for passing GO, three doubles in a row to Jail, mortgages at half value, last player left wins.  
- Who made it  
  - Sculptured Software  developed it, and Parker Brothers published it in North America in May 1991\. (Wikipedia)  
  - Tomy, which sold the board game in Japan, published the Famicom version on November 1, 1991\.  
  - The versions: NES, Famicom, Game Boy, Super NES and Genesis.  
- The pitch  
  - Monopoly with a computer Banker. The manual says to play a practice game with humans, then take on eight fast-playing, sometimes ruthless computer opponents.  
  - The box included a poster printed with every Title Deed, to cut apart and hold like real cards.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The bank Vault for setting up the game is a cute interface  
  - The computers have portraits to choose from, but do they have different playstyles?  
    - Arthur, Gertrude, Erwin, Maude, Carmen, Isaac, Penelope and Ollie. Arthur is an orphanage-raised millionaire who drives hard bargains, Maude is a tycoon's daughter who wastes money shamelessly, and Ollie cannot say no to anyone who needs cash.&nbsp;  
  - What’s your go to symbol?  
- Two to eight players, any mix of human and computer  
  - A menu toggle swaps a seat between Computer and Human mid-game.  
- Monopoly with a time limit\!  
  - Play untimed, or set a timer from 15 minutes to several hours. The game ends the moment time runs out. (HonestGamers)  
  - Hurry/Relax changes the pace, and short games can deal two random deeds to every player to skip the slow opening.  
- A Game Editor and eight Wheel and Deal scenarios  
  - Pre-assign cash, properties, tokens and buildings. Presets run from The Big Boys to Building Shortage.  
  - You can edit nearly everything about the game. I thought it was gonna let me change the board. You can change how much money each person has down to the dollar. So naturally I gave myself 1 more dollar than the computers  
- The computer is the Banker and the referee  
  - It handles every payment, deed, rent and auction

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Were you expecting to see hands? They’re committing to this being a tabletop game. I think at that point add a table and some drinks that can spill onto the board  
- The computers play FAST\!  
  - Computer players blast through menus, which makes their trades hard to follow, and the jail-release voice clip is unsettling and comically bad.&nbsp;  
- The core loop of a turn  
  - Press A to roll, and the token moves itself. Doubles mean another roll.  
  - Land on an unowned space and press A to buy or B to send it to auction. Land on someone's property and rent is paid automatically. Chance and Community Chest get their own card screens.  
  - SELECT opens the Main Menu: Properties (Trade, Mortgage, Un-mortgage), Buildings, Status (Portfolio, Assets, Deeds, Board, Sequence) and Game Control.  
  - Your cash shows at the start of every turn, with the houses and hotels left in the bank. A bankrupt player retires and the last one standing wins. (booklet)  
- Saving and quitting  
  - There is no save or password. End Game asks "Are you sure?", names a winner, shows everyone's assets, and then you turn off the console. A timer is the practical way to finish in one sitting.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- Trading by asking what the computer wants  
  - The trade system seemed somewhat grounded in reality?  
  - On the pink trade screen you can leave your side blank and press Yes, and the computer names the deeds it wants.  
  - It nags you for deeds, though it tries only one trade a round. The reviewer called it workable but clunky.  
- Auctions  
  - A voiced auctioneer sets every bid, calls going, going, going, then sold, and you can only accept a bid with A. You cannot bid more cash than you hold, so you mortgage mid-auction.  
  - If the bank runs out of houses or hotels, each unit goes to auction, and hotels take priority. (booklet)  
- Rough edges  
  - Its hard to know what properties you own and others own in the moment to moment  
- Free Parking pays nothing  
  - The manual says you get no money, property or reward. The jackpot many families play is not in the official rules and is not on the cartridge.

## V. History and Development

- Sculptured Software  
  - Founded in Salt Lake City in 1984 and incorporated in Utah on July 12, 1985, it made its name porting arcade games to home consoles.  
  - Its other NES work includes Roger Clemens' MVP Baseball in 1991 and Tecmo NBA Basketball in 1992\. Later came Super Star Wars and the Super NES ports of Doom and Mortal Kombat. (Coleka, Wikipedia)  
  - Acclaim bought it in October 1995 in an all-stock deal reportedly worth about \$30 million.  
  - Parker Brothers used the team again for Clue, on Super NES in July 1992 and Genesis in January 1993\.  
- The credits  
  - Bill Williams programmed the NES version and Paul Webb composed the music.  
- The license  
  - Parker Brothers began in Salem, Massachusetts in 1883\. General Mills bought it in 1968, Tonka acquired it in 1987, and Hasbro bought Tonka in 1991 for roughly half a billion dollars. (Wikipedia)  
  - So this is a Tonka-era Parker Brothers game in the year Hasbro took over. The booklet names Parker Brothers as a division of Tonka Corporation in Beverly, Massachusetts, and was printed in Japan.  
- The computer ancestors  
  - Leisure Genius published the first licensed computer Monopoly in 1984, for the Commodore 64\. It briefly knocked Ghostbusters off the top of the Boots C64 chart. It takes about 3-5 seconds just for the dice to roll.

## VI. Music and Presentation

- Graphics  
  - The board matches the real one. The view switches between the whole board from above and a zoom on the space you landed on, with big, clean menu text. (Just Games Retro)  
  - A deed diagram above the board is colored if you own a property, gray if someone else does, and empty if it is unowned.  
  - The animations are the charm: a fist shaking the dice, a dog and a car on the move, a cash register swallowing rent, a rubber stamp on each purchase, and jail scenes.  
  - One reviewer calls the board too plain and some animations choppy. The tokens are the most detailed thing on screen. (HonestGamers)  
- Music and sound  
  - There is no constant music. Short themes mark startup, bankruptcy, game end and the options menu.

## VIII. Sequels, Spinoffs and Comparison Games

- Two other Super Famicom  
  - Monopoly (Super Famicom, Tomy, March 5, 1993\)  
    - A separate Japan-only game by Ape and CreamSoft, not a port.  
  - The Monopoly Game 2 (Super Famicom, Tomy, March 31, 1995\)  
    - Developed by Tomcat System, and Famicom Tsushin scored it 30 out of 40\.  
- Same-era ports  
  - Game Boy (December 1991\)  
    - A handheld version, with a Game Link cable to connect two units. Nintendo Power gave it 3.725 out of 5\.  
  - Genesis (1992)  
    - Up to eight players on one controller, animated scenes at each destination, and an End Game that crowns the highest net worth. Sega-16 gave it 6 out of 10\.  
  - Super NES (September 1992\)  
    - A 16-bit version with music by Nu Romantic Productions, and a GameRankings average of 50 percent.  
- Later Monopolies  
  - Monopoly Deluxe (Virgin Games, 1992, DOS and Windows): the first official Windows version, driven by mouse.  
  - Monopoly Tycoon (Deep Red Games, 2001, Windows): turns it into a city/business management game. Touted as Published by the makers of Roller Coaster Tycoon, but the developers were the developers of ***Risk II*** which I played a lot on PC growing up.  
- Modern comparison games  
  - Monopoly Plus (The one Joe and I played) (Asobo Studio, 2014, PS3, PS4, Xbox 360, Xbox One, PC)  
    - It adds the My Monopoly expansion, which lets you name the board spaces whatever you want.  
    - The 2017 Switch version, from Ubisoft, rattles the dice through Joy-Con HD Rumble, where the NES showed a fist shaking them.  
  - Monopoly Go\! (Scopely, April 11, 2023, iOS and Android)  
    - A mobile game that passed \$1 billion in revenue by November 2023 and \$2 billion by March 2024\.  
    - It shares the theme and the dice, but you start owning every property, spend money on landmarks on themed boards like Tokyo, and run bank heists on other players.  
    - Its a gacha game with limited daily dice rolls, which you can buy more of, where the NES let you roll as long as the game ran.  
  - Mario Party Superstars (NDcube, Nintendo, October 29, 2021, Switch)  
    - A board game built for a living room, with five remade N64 boards and 100 minigames.  
    - It shares the dice and the board, but the winner is whoever collects the most stars, not the last player solvent.  
    - The signature moment is buying a star from Toadette for 20 coins, with a random minigame after every round.  
  - \- Monopoly DEAL:&nbsp;  
    - A fast-paced, cutthroat card game where you collect property sets, charge rent, steal properties, and mess with your opponents using action cards. The first player to collect three complete property sets of different colors wins. Games typically take around 15 minutes.&nbsp;  
  - \- Monopoly Millionaire DEAL:&nbsp;  
    - A variation that shifts the focus from collecting property sets to accumulating wealth. You still use action cards and interact with opponents, but the goal is to be the first player to amass \$1 million. It keeps the same quick, competitive feel, with more emphasis on money than monopolies.

## IX. Essential Games List

- Case for Essential  
  - It does what a conversion should: it removes the chores and keeps the game, with no Banker to appoint and no rules argument to referee.  
  - It is cheap, about \$8 loose and about \$25 complete. (Gameventory)  
- Case against Essential  
  - It gives up the best part of Monopoly, haggling across a table, for a menu and a computer that nags for deeds.  
  - On your turn you roll and the game does the rest, so there is little for a player to do.  
- Suggested verdict: not essential, but the best argument that a cartridge can make Monopoly easier to play.
