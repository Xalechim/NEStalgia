## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: shoot Detroit, arrest Detroit, collect the drugs
  - You are RoboCop, chasing the drug Nuke and its dealer Cain across seven side-scrolling stages. Each level has two quotas: destroy Nuke canisters and arrest criminals. Miss either quota and you do not advance.
- Straight off the 1990 film
  - RoboCop 2, the movie: Irvin Kershner directing, Frank Miller on the script, Nuke as the new designer drug, Cain's brain eventually loaded into a giant robot. The game covers the plot beats loosely. Levels include riverside warehouses, a chemical plant, the Nuke factory, and the Civic Centrum for the finale. (MobyGames)
- Who actually made it
  - Data East published in North America, Ocean published in Europe, and the development credit points to Painting by Numbers, a studio working under the Ocean umbrella. Music by Keith Tinman, who also did the Game Boy and Commodore 64 versions. NES release April 1991. The Amiga original landed in the UK in November 1990.
- Every version of this game is a different game
  - The Amiga and ZX Spectrum versions mix platforming with first-person shooting galleries and a chip-arranging memory puzzle. The arcade version, built by Data East, is a belt-scrolling brawler. The NES and Game Boy versions are closer to straight shoot 'em ups. (Wikipedia)
- The simplest way to frame it for listeners
  - This is the RoboCop game where the license and the design are actively fighting each other. You are playing a character defined by being unstoppable, in a game that makes you fragile, and the objective is a percentage.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- The 60 percent rule
  - You need 60 percent of the Nuke canisters and 60 percent of the arrestable criminals in a stage to move on. You are not told what 60 percent is in raw numbers until you reach the end of the level. (Questicle) You also cannot backtrack to look for more.
- Arrests are a mechanic, and they are genuinely awkward
  - Arrestable enemies flash and trigger an audio cue. You arrest one by walking into him. (MobyGames, GameFAQs) The problem is obvious: this is a game where you are otherwise shooting everything that moves, and an arrest target walks at you like a regular thug. Questicle's line on it is that RoboCop is programmed to shoot anything that walks, so staying your hand is harder than it sounds.
- The failure state is a shooting gallery
  - Miss a quota and you get bounced to a first-person firing range, a Hogan's Alley style stage with cardboard targets and a time limit. Hit the target quota and the game lets you continue. Fail it and you replay the level you just failed. (MobyGames, Questicle)
- Secrets are signposted, sort of
  - The level intro text drops a hint about where a secret might be. Secrets include bonus canister stages, a hidden shooting gallery, a sliding-tile puzzle of Alex Murphy's face that restores RoboCop's human memory, and a three-number safe combination belonging to Cain. (MobyGames)
- HIGH SCORE BOARD
  - The cartridge is booby-trapped against the Game Genie. On startup the game sums a block of zero-page RAM against matching values in ROM. If everything cancels to zero, which is exactly what happens when the Game Genie's setup routine has just run, the check never exits and the game hangs in an infinite loop forever. A licensed cartridge deliberately bricking itself to stop you cheating is on-brand for a game about a cop. Elsewhere in the same ROM, at the tail of Bank 6, somebody left behind the placeholder string "hello this is a bit of text," which never appears anywhere in the game. (TCRF via search results, confirmed by GameFAQs)

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Basic controls
  - A: jump. B: attack. D-pad: move. Start: pause. Select: not used. (GameFAQs)
- Seven levels, side-scrolling, forward-only
  - The box promises seven levels of sideways scrolling. Progression is gated by the two percentage quotas rather than by reaching an exit.
- The combat loop
  - Shoot with the pistol, or punch enemies who get close. Weapon pickups upgrade bullet strength or add new firing patterns. There are health restores and short invincibility pickups. (MobyGames)
- Jet platforms
  - Several levels have flying jet platforms that you steer manually to cross gaps and reach higher areas. (MobyGames)
- The final boss is a three-round fight
  - RoboCop 2 charges you immediately, fires missile waves, a bullet spray you duck under, and pink blobs. Both of you start at 99 health. Phase two: only his legs are vulnerable. Phase three: only his head. Health partially restores between phases. (GameFAQs)

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- Hidden quota math is the core frustration
  - You can finish a stage feeling like you swept it and still be told you came up short. Because you cannot go back, a missed canister early is a failed level you do not learn about until the end.
- RoboCop dies to metal
  - Questicle's complaint, and it is a fair one: RoboCop takes damage from touching things a cyborg made of metal should shrug off.
- Control feel was a contemporary complaint, not just a modern one
  - Nintendo Power's April 1991 coverage noted that RoboCop is initially difficult to control. (Wikipedia) This was flagged across the computer versions too. Amiga Format and Amiga Power both called out the controls.
- The memory puzzle is the best idea in the game
  - Solving a sliding-tile portrait of Alex Murphy restores RoboCop's human memories. That is a genuinely good marriage of theme and mechanic, and it is buried in an optional secret.
- The Game Over screen is doing a lot
  - "RoboCop is taken away for calibration. But he will try again!" (Questicle) Losing is framed as a maintenance appointment.

## V. History & Development

- Ocean bought RoboCop at the script stage
  - Ocean licensed RoboCop from Orion Pictures before the first film was even finished, then sub-licensed it to Data East for arcades. That is why the same property produced completely unrelated games on different platforms.
- The team worked off an unfinished movie
  - Special FX, building the Amiga version, was given a pre-release cut of RoboCop 2 with several scenes missing, plus a preliminary script and character stills. (Wikipedia) Programming started months before the film opened.
- The NES version is a different lineage entirely
  - The NES RoboCop 2 shares its music driver with other Ocean NES titles. The driver carries a hidden Jonathan Dunn and Ocean Software copyright string inside the ROM. (TCRF via search results) So a British-made audio engine is running under a Data East-published American cartridge.
- HG101's blunt summary of the whole situation
  - Ocean held the license for every subsequent console and computer RoboCop, and none of them were very good.

## VI. Reception & Legacy

- The computer versions were adored, the NES version was not
  - The ZX Spectrum version scored in the 90s at Crash, Sinclair User, and Your Sinclair, and won Crash's 1990 Readers' Award for Best Game Overall with 21 percent of the vote. By April 1991 it was the second best-selling game in the UK, behind the Turtles. (Wikipedia)
- Meanwhile, in Japan
  - Famitsu's four-reviewer cross review scored the NES version 4, 6, 7, and 5. That is 22 out of 40. (Wikipedia)
- Contemporary NES coverage was mild
  - Nintendo Power ran it in April 1991 and flagged the controls. A Brazilian magazine, VideoGame, gave the NES version 4 out of 5. That is the only strongly positive NES-specific review in the record.
- Modern retrospectives are harsh
  - Questicle's verdict on the NES game is close to pure vitriol, aimed squarely at the quota system and the arrest mechanic.
- No re-release
  - This one has not been reissued. The RoboCop games that get modern attention are the arcade original and Rogue City, not this cartridge.

## VII. Pop Culture

- The movie's arcade scene is a Data East ad
  - RoboCop 2 has a scene where RoboCop walks into an arcade to lean on a crooked cop. Every cabinet on screen is a Data East game: Bad Dudes, Sly Spy, Heavy Barrel, Midnight Resistance, The Real Ghostbusters, Slap Shot, and Kung Fu Master. Midnight Resistance is about fighting a drug operation, which makes the placement funnier. In some shots the Ghostbusters cabinets are displaying screenshots from entirely different games.
- Frank Miller wrote this movie
  - The RoboCop 2 screenplay came from the Dark Knight Returns and Sin City writer, heavily rewritten by the studio. Miller's original version was later adapted as a comic, RoboCop: Frank Miller's Unproduced Screenplay.
- Nuke and the drug war
  - The film is a 1990 satire of the crack era and privatized policing. The game turns that satire into a collectible. You are literally scoring points by picking up drug bottles, which is a strange artifact of translating a Verhoeven-universe story to a family console.
- RoboCop was everywhere and rated R for none of it
  - Action figures, a Saturday morning cartoon, a cereal-adjacent merchandising blitz, all built off one of the most violent films of its era. The NES cartridge is part of that machine: the property sold the game, not the other way around.

## VIII. Sequels and Spinoffs

- The NES RoboCop shelf
  - RoboCop (1989, NES): different developer, Sakata SAS, and famously RoboCop cannot jump at all. (HG101) RoboCop 2 (1991, NES): this one. RoboCop 3 (1992, NES): Probe, published by Ocean. RoboCop Versus The Terminator (1993, NES): Interplay. Four RoboCop cartridges on one console, no two of them alike.
- The arcade RoboCop 2 is a separate game
  - Data East built an arcade RoboCop 2 in 1991, a belt-scrolling brawler with separate buttons for shooting left and right. HG101 calls it fun but shallow, and The One called it an uninspired follow-up. It charted as the 24th most successful table cabinet in Japan in April 1991.
- Comparison games
  - RoboCop (NES): the direct predecessor, worth playing back to back just to hear how different the two design philosophies are. Batman (NES, Sunsoft): the counterexample, a licensed 1989 movie tie-in that is genuinely excellent. Darkman or Dick Tracy (NES): Ocean-adjacent licensed titles from the same window that show the house style.

## IX. Essential Games List Vote

- Case for Essential
  - The quota system is a perfect fossil of a specific NES-era design mistake, and a game that fails this legibly is more useful to understand than a competent one.
  - Supporting points: The memory-puzzle secret is a real idea, and rare in a tie-in. The anti-Game Genie hang is one of the stranger things anyone shipped on the console. It is a genuine artifact of the licensing boom at its peak.
- Case against Essential
  - The central mechanic hides its own win condition from you, and no amount of RoboCop affection survives failing a level for a reason the game never showed you.
  - Supporting points: The arrest mechanic fights the shooting mechanic on purpose and neither one wins. Contemporary reviewers flagged the controls in 1991, so this is not hindsight. If you want a good RoboCop 2, the Spectrum and Amiga versions are the ones that won awards, and neither is on this console.
