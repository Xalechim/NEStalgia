# 457 - Nobunaga's Ambition II

- Return to a world of loyalty and honor

## I. Setup & Premise

- The pitch: be a warlord, unify Japan, do your own bookkeeping
  - You pick a daimyo in Sengoku-era Japan and try to conquer all 38 fiefs.
  - Conquest is maybe a third of the job.
  - The rest is taxes, rice harvests, flood control, and keeping your peasants from rioting.
  - Official framing from Koei: command armies, defend against ninja assassins, lay siege to fortresses, negotiate alliances, and also handle typhoons, epidemics, and famine before your people revolt.
- The title is a lie, sort of
  - In Japan this is Nobunaga no Yabou: Sengoku Gun'yuuden, "Tales of the Sengoku Warlords." It is the third game in the series, not the second. Koei called it II in the US because it was the second one we got.

## II. What Makes It Stand Out

- Generals are the whole reason this game exists
  - The first Nobunaga's Ambition had provinces and armies but no people running them. This one adds roughly 400 real historical samurai, each with their own stats and portrait.
  - Koei borrowed the idea from their own Romance of the Three Kingdoms series and it changed the franchise permanently.
- Samurai are RPG characters
  - Age, Political Influence, War Ability, Charisma, Ambition, Loyalty, number of men, troop skill, arms level, and unit type.
  - Ages and lifespans are based on real history. If a man died at 47 of illness, he will probably die around 47 in your game.
  - Low loyalty means he might defect mid-battle or start a civil war against you.
- Two scenarios, two completely different games
  - Warlord Rivalry, March 1560: 28 daimyo, nobody holds more than four fiefs, wide open.
  - Nobunaga's Ambition, March 1582: Oda already owns most of the map and you are either him or the resistance.
- It looks noticeably better than the first one
  - Cleaner graphics, brighter colors, small animations attached to actions like training troops.
  - it is a glossy coat of paint on the original, more update than sequel.
- BUG
  - Use the View Samurai command on any fief and the fief number displays one lower than it actually is. Kozuke, province 2, shows up as "1 Kozuke." Echigo, province 1, shows up as "0 Echigo," a province that does not exist. Use View Daimyo instead and the number is correct.

## III. Gameplay Structure

- Basic controls
  - Select and Start: nothing. They do nothing at all.
- Body Points are the real currency
  - Every samurai has Body Points, derived from Political Influence, capped at 200, regenerating at 30 percent of that stat per month.
  - Every action costs BP. Building is 50, recruiting a samurai is 70, training is 90, arranging a marriage is 130.
  - So your turn is not limited by gold. It is limited by how much attention your guys have left.

## IV. Unique Gameplay Elements

- Charge versus Normal attack is a life-or-death choice
  - Normal attack: if you win, you capture the enemy samurai alive. Charge: if you win, he dies. If you lose, your guy dies.
- Prisoner management
  - After you win, every captured samurai gets one of three options: behead, recruit, or release. Recruit a man and the guide's advice is to strip his troops until his loyalty is bought back up. Release him and he just turns up in someone else's army.
- The errand-boy disposal trick
  - Got a samurai with terrible stats you want gone? Dismissing him means he joins a rival.
  - The workaround from a guide: hand him a single soldier, send him to war, and have him Charge so he dies instead of being captured. Charming game.
- The rice market exploit
  - Selling a large amount of food drops the market price by 0.1. Buying raises it by 0.1. So you sell your whole stock, the price drops, you buy it all back for the same money plus extra, and repeat. Given enough Body Points you can farm free rice out of thin air. (GameFAQs)
- Fortify Castle is a trap
  - It is the one Build option that does not raise happiness. It only matters if you are actually besieged. The guide's position: skip it entirely and buy more men.
- Your daimyo will die
  - Everyone ages in March. When your daimyo dies you pick a successor and keep going. Worth knowing: when any samurai dies, the soldiers under his command vanish with him. You cannot reassign them first unless you saw it coming.

## V. History & Development

- Koei was a Japanese PC company first
  - Yoichi and Keiko Erikawa founded Koei. Yoichi produces under the name Kou Shibusawa. (GameFAQs trivia) The Historical Simulation line, Nobunaga's Ambition, Romance of the Three Kingdoms, Genghis Khan, was built for Japanese 8-bit and 16-bit computers, then ported down to consoles.
- The Famicom port is a downgrade in scope, not depth
  - The PC-88 original came out December 1988 and spread across MSX, PC-98, X1, X68000, FM-7, FM Towns, and DOS. Tohoku and Kyushu were cut from the map, which is why the game is 38 fiefs and not the whole country.
- The NES had a small, strange strategy shelf and Koei owned most of it
  - Genghis Khan, Bandit Kings of Ancient China, Romance of the Three Kingdoms, L'Empereur, Gemfire, plus both Nobunaga games. These were expensive cartridges aimed at an audience that mostly did not exist on the NES.

## VI. Reception & Legacy

- The recurring complaint is the endgame
  - Strong start, dead middle. The AI rarely attacks a well-defended fief, so a competent player can hit a stalemate where nothing happens. Once you are big, conquering the remaining 30 fiefs is administration, not strategy.
- It is back in print, right now
  - Hamster released Console Archives Nobunaga's Ambition II for PS5 and Switch 2 on September 10, 2026, at $7.99, with the NES version's English text included.
  - The first Nobunaga's Ambition got the same treatment in March 2026. These are emulation reissues with save states, rewind, screen filters, and remappable buttons.

## VII. Pop Culture

- Koei Tecmo eventually turned their own strategy roster into the Dynasty Warriors action series, which is how most Western players met these names.
- This game teaches real history
  - The 1582 scenario starts the same year as the Honnoji Incident, the betrayal that killed Nobunaga.
- The Sengoku strategy game as a Japanese cultural staple
  - Nobunaga's Ambition is closer to an annual institution in Japan than a game series. It hit 40 years in 2023. Yoko Kanno, later famous for Cowboy Bebop, composed for the series early on.
- The economics behind the cartridge
  - Koei games were premium-priced strategy titles on a console built for platformers. That business model, sell fewer copies at a higher price to a devoted niche.

## VIII. Sequels and Spinoffs

- Where it sits in the series
  - Nobunaga's Ambition (1983, Japan only)
  - Nobunaga's Ambition: Bushou Fuunroku (1990, famicom only), released here on the SNES as Lord of Darkness
  - The series is still running. Nobunaga's Ambition: Awakening released in 2022
- Spinoff worth mentioning
  - Inindo: Way of the Ninja (SNES) is an RPG set in the same world where you are trying to kill Nobunaga rather than be him.
  - Sid Meier's Civilization: arrived the same year, from the other direction, and shows what happens when this design gets a mouse and a Western audience.

## IX. Essential Games List Vote

- Case for Essential
  - This is the NES library's deepest strategy game, and you cannot understand the console's full range without acknowledging that Koei shipped a 400-character historical simulation onto it in 1991.
- The two-scenario structure gives it real replay value.
- Case against Essential
  - You are asking listeners to read menus on a television for 40 hours, and the game solves itself once you understand that bigger armies win.
- No NES-specific reason to play it. The computer versions are better and came first. The endgame stalls out into pure administration. Essential for the NES should probably mean essential ON the NES, and this was a port down, not a game built for the hardware.
